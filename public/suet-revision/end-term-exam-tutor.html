Session 4 · Prof. Ram Nidumolu · Strategy

Three takeaways to remember in ten years

Business models for climate change, payment for ecosystem services, market design and stakeholder management. The professor framed the session around three takeaways and a closed-book case write-up.

Takeaway 1

Focus

Use the four lenses (business value, stakeholder influence, science and technology, purpose). Only issues visible through all four become priorities. Strategy is focusing limited resources.

Takeaway 2

Innovate

Move through the five stages: proactive compliance, sustainable value chains, sustainable products, new business models, next-practice platforms.

Takeaway 3

Build a platform

A platform uses same-side and cross-side network effects to aggregate many small changes. Ant Forest: small changes × 700m users = big impact.

The 10-minute closed-book write-up (slides 4 to 7)

  1. What were the key elements of the Ant Forest business philosophy and model in incentivising individual users to follow a low-carbon, eco-friendly lifestyle? (the one actually asked)
  2. Who and how were the key partners (specialised, low-carbon, charitable, others) leveraged by Ant Forest?
  3. How did Ant Forest promote biodiverse regions, eco-friendly forest-agriculture and employment through the lifestyles it promoted?

Answers are on pages 4E and 4F.

4B · Getting Strategic About Sustainability (HBR 2025)

Four lenses: finding your focus

Strategy is about choosing what not to do. The materiality matrix produces a bloated list: in 256 sustainability reports from 2022, firms named 11.8 top-priority issues on average (range 2 to 43). People can hold 3 to 5 items in mind; experts advise no more than about 6.

Issues in all four lenses become the firm's critical sustainability issues. The lenses balance outside-in vs inside-out views and perception vs reality.

Points from the article

  • Business value is also called single materiality: apolitical and financial; dominant in SEC, SASB and most reporting frameworks. "Submerged value" (Aronson): talent retention in pharma can be worth billions. Walmart's 2006 "zero waste" goal served everyday low prices.
  • Stakeholder influence: anticipate what stakeholders will do, not only say; soft law becomes hard law. Eversource and the activist group Mothers Out Front: methane leaks led to measurement, legislation and district geothermal. UBS: "prefinancial information".
  • Science & technology: a neutral fact base. Nike got 49 stakeholder surveys yielding 24 issues. Stakeholders can be wrong (GMOs, nuclear).
  • Purpose: Costco asked whether it treated supply-chain workers like its own. Purpose-driven market shapers: Novo Nordisk, Tesla, Grameen, Patagonia, Stonyfield.

KDP result

Three priorities instead of 21 medium- and high-impact issues: (1) eliminate waste while keeping single-serve convenience, (2) responsible coffee sourcing, (3) health and well-being (Dr Pepper sugars). Innovation: plastic-free, compostable K-Rounds (2024).

How the professor applied the lenses

  • Business value: everyone starts here (cost, efficiency, ROI), but it is not enough.
  • Purpose: Harley-Davidson (page 4D).
  • Stakeholders: the Maggi noodles crisis; ignoring social-media voices cost an estimated ₹2,000 crore.
  • Science & technology: Microsoft missed cloud and AI by chasing the quarter, then Satya Nadella invested (OpenAI). "Apply these lenses to life too."

4C · Why Sustainability Is Now the Key Driver of Innovation (HBR 2009)

Five stages of sustainable innovation

Sustainability = innovation. Going green lowers costs and raises revenues, so it should be the touchstone for all innovation. The research base was 30 large corporations; early movers build competencies rivals struggle to match.

Slide 11: "Types of Sustainable Innovations". Stages 1 and 2 add value through risk management and efficiency; stages 3 to 5 are business growth opportunities.

  • 2: Walmart's 1,000 Chinese suppliers; FedEx Fuel Sense; IBM and AT&T telecommuting; Cisco returns: reuse 5% to 45%, costs −40%, $100m contribution.
  • 3: P&G Tide Coldwater; Clorox Green Works (40% of a $200m market).
  • 4: FedEx-Kinko's; Waste Management's Green Squad; Calera cement.
  • 5: smart grid; waterless detergent; rice without water.

From class discussion

  • Why comply early? Lower future cost on your own timeline; up the learning curve first; first-mover differentiation; reputation; shape the regulation. Student examples: Flipkart and Zomato EV fleets, ExxonMobil meeting the strictest country's law everywhere, Airbus certification, pharma plants meeting US standards for India, Tata's early Provident Fund.
  • Cisco's three streams from returned routers: refurbish for a secondary market, harvest components, give them to sales staff as training labs (raised the top line).
  • Article's "few simple rules": don't start from the present; learning precedes investment; stay wedded to the goal, adjust tactics; build collaborative capacity; use a global presence (emerging markets) to experiment.
Professor's sample MCQ: "What is NOT a reason that manufacturers in sustainable supply-chain initiatives encourage suppliers to adopt eco-friendly practices?" Answer: to simplify consumer-facing sales and marketing processes. Sustainable inputs, resource efficiency and supplier compliance are stage 2; consumer marketing belongs to products and business models (stages 3 to 4).

4D · Tools and purpose in action

LCA, Tide Coldwater and Harley-Davidson

Life-cycle analysis (slide 14)

An approach to measure and inventory the full range of environmental impacts associated with the inputs and outputs of raw materials, energy and waste across a product's life: acquisition of raw materials, material manufacture, final product fabrication, packaging and distribution, consumer use and disposal.

P&G's chart (Len Sauers, head of sustainability): product groups × life-cycle stage × energy used. The tallest bar, roughly equal to all others combined, was consumer use of laundry detergent, because of heating water.

Tide Coldwater: a triple win

Redesign the detergent to dissolve in cold water. Customers pay lower electricity bills, the company gains a better product, the environment avoids the energy. HBR: US households spend 3% of annual electricity on heating wash water; cold-water washing could save 80 billion kWh and 34 million tons of CO₂. Launched 2005 (Ariel Cool Clean in Europe).

Professor's sample MCQ: P&G developed Tide Coldwater after undertaking: Life Cycle Analysis (not digitising the customer journey, design thinking, or a supplier sustainability audit).

Harley-Davidson (slides 15 to 16)

"H-D's heritage is its brand image: the freedom to ride." Earlier consultants pitching carbon, water and energy failed with riders. Chairman, President & CEO Keith Wandell: "Our purpose is clear: we fulfill dreams of personal freedom. And from that our sustainability vision is simple: we preserve and renew the freedom to ride." Freedom needs the open outdoors; sustainability preserves the outdoors. That emotional hook made sustainability meaningful.

"Creating shared value." Purpose for the growth pillar: grow revenues profitably by strengthening the brand for core customers in developed markets while renewing it for customers in other markets.

Covers S4 slides: 14 (LCA, P&G chart), 15 (H-D quote), 16 (sustainable products for a bike company).
4E · Slides 17 to 32

Indian examples for each stage

The deck illustrated every stage with company cases. An MCQ can name a company and ask for its stage, so learn which section each sat in.

Stage 1 · Compliance as opportunity

ITC Paperkraft (slide 18)

2007-08: India debated banning elemental chlorine in paper bleaching (toxic organo-chlorine by-products). In 2008 ITC launched Paperkraft Premium Business Paper using ozone-treated Elemental Chlorine Free (ECF) technology, virtually eliminating toxins.

Nerolac Paints (slide 19)

CSE lab tests in 2008 and 2009 found high lead in household paints; 72% of samples exceeded the BIS limit (lead harms brain and nervous system). Nerolac launched lead-free paint in 2009, before the Government's 2016 "Regulation on Lead content in Household and Decorative Paints Rules".

Stage 2 · Sustainable value chains

Mahindra & Mahindra (slide 21)

Suppliers classed hazardous/non-hazardous and dangerous/non-dangerous; EMS and OHSAS certification (fewer supplier accidents); reusable packaging at supplier sites; training and awareness (96 suppliers trained in 2018-19); Supplier Sustainability Meets.

Tata Motors (slide 22)

DJSI sustainability leader 2018; Asia's best report design (ASRA). Green Dealership spreads energy conservation and lower GHG through channel partners. Value from Hazardous Waste: paint sludge turned into secondary paints, saving ₹20 crore in FY20.

Tata Steel HIsarna (slide 23)

Piloted a process (Hisarna = Celtic "iron" + "smelt") that cuts energy use and CO₂ by about a fifth vs the blast furnace, skips pre-processing steps, and could cut CO₂ by up to 80%. TERI: steel CO₂ could rise from 242 Mt to 837 Mt as India's consumption quadruples to ~500 Mt.

Stage 3 · Sustainable products

Havells (slide 25)

Replaced radioactive Krypton-85 (used to ignite arc discharge lamps; linked to cancers, thyroid, organ disorders) with Argon across its CMI lighting range. No Havells product now has radioactive components.

Godrej Appliances (slide 26)

Replaced thermocol (EPS, petroleum-based, non-biodegradable) with paper honeycomb packaging: under 1 kg CO₂ per product vs 4.2 kg for EPS, cheaper, less storage. First white-goods brand to do so (since 2019).

Log9 Materials (slide 27)

Bangalore nanotech: aluminium-air batteries and fuel cells; lithium-titanium-oxide EV batteries (RapidX: under 15 min charge for two-wheelers, 35-40 min for three- and four-wheelers; claims 9x life, 15+ years). Recycling with Metastable Materials, over 90% recovery.

Stages 4 and 5 · Models and platforms

Phool (slide 29, stage 4)

Kanpur start-up (2017, Ankit Agarwal and Prateek Kumar) that "flower-cycles" temple waste (including Kashi Vishwanath) from three cities, averting about 13 tonnes of flowers and chemicals from rivers daily. Self-help-group women make charcoal-free incense, essential oils and organic fertiliser; R&D on Fleather, a vegan alternative to animal leather.

Michelin Fleet Solutions (slide 30, stage 4)

Tyre lease programmes and pay by the mile: fleets buy tyre performance as a service; energy-efficient tyres save fuel and cut emissions.

Tata Power EZ Charge (slide 32, stage 5)

Plan for 25,000 EV charging points in five years; adding 21,000 home and 240 bus chargers to 2,400+ existing; EZ Charge app to locate, pay and plan routes, with central monitoring; skills 3,000 youth a year in renewables.

4F · Case: CEIBS CB0130 (Meng Rui & Qiong Zhu, 2021)

Ant Forest: how it hooked individual users

Alipay's in-app environmental mini-program (August 2016) turns low-carbon actions into "green energy" points that grow a virtual tree; enough points and Alipay plants a real one in a desertification-prone area. Answer to write-up question 1.

Slide 35, from case Exhibit 3. The emissions algorithm was co-developed with the China Beijing Environmental Exchange; a specialised third party sets points per tree from CO₂ captured when grown.

Elements that incentivised users

  • Make it easy: many everyday actions already on Alipay earn points: walking, subway, bills, appointments, e-invoices.
  • Make it a game: points expire after 3 days (daily habit); steal friends' points, water their trees; leaderboards; people set 7 a.m. alarms to stop theft.
  • Make it real and visible: virtual tree becomes a real tree; the "scarecrow" icon shows photos; certificates under "Achievements" visible to friends. This fixed the trust gap: people wanted to plant trees but did not know how and feared misuse of donations.
  • Keep it fresh: more species from May 2017; co-planting (pool points with family, classmates, colleagues) when costly trees put users off.
  • Make it social: Spring Festival 2017 Wufu five-blessings campaign; 60m users in 3 months, 200m by end-February 2017, +100m a year, 500m by August 2019.
  • Survey motivators: sense of achievement from a real tree 83.3%; doing good 66.7%; interacting with friends 30.8%; gamified design 19.2%; curiosity 16.7%; friends use it 11.5%; proving self-worth 7.7%.

Why Alipay built it

  • Alipay was a payments tool; WeChat (1.2bn MAUs) had social stickiness. Alipay's social feature "Circles" failed.
  • The original plan, personal carbon accounts tradable on a carbon market, was far-fetched in 2016: individuals could not access carbon markets, so reductions had no value to users. Product manager Zu Wang's team chose tree planting.
  • Result: logins per user rose from 2.4/day (2016) to 4.0/day (January 2019); one user stopped using Apple Pay for Alipay.
  • Alipay: 1.2bn users, 53.6% of China's third-party mobile payments (2019) vs Tenpay 39.5%.
  • A typical game lasts 1 to 2 years; Ant Forest escaped that because it was a real-world initiative, not just a game. Zu: young people want participation and a real-world connection, not just donating money.
  • Behaviour spillover: 100m+ customers bought eco-products on Taobao and Tmall (Jan-Oct 2017); reusable bag sales more than doubled in 2018. Two-thirds of surveyed users said they led greener lives; 57.7% more aware; 48.7% more attentive to charity.
  • Awards: UNEP case study (April 2019); UN Champions of the Earth (September 2019).

Biodiversity and eco-agriculture

  • Pingwu, Sichuan (China's largest panda population), May 2018: protection plots claimed in 53 days; honey sold out (10,000 jars) in 40 minutes, then in 1 minute; AI beekeeping; EU certification; raw honey prices +60% (over ¥200/kg online); ~¥50m a year income. Model: raise the profile of protected land, add smart tech, then contract farming (also vinegar, mushrooms, black rice).
  • Sea buckthorn (from November 2018): 23m users, 10m+ shrubs over 60+ km² by 2019; farmers earn ¥1,000 per 666 m², five times traditional farming; ¥40,000-50,000 per winter harvest in Qingshuihe; juice: 1m boxes in 163 minutes.
  • Why it matters (class): livelihoods stop poaching and protect habitats; mixed species build ecosystems that survive; bees are a keystone species.

PICPOP business model (slides 39 to 40)

  • Purpose & positioning: encourage low-carbon lifestyles; plant real trees in arid regions; a digital environmental initiative inside Alipay (distinct from WeChat's social position).
  • Innovation model: digital tech and gamification turn actions into "green energy" points; tech partners give real-time tracking and analytics.
  • Customer model: Alipay users, environmentally conscious Chinese consumers; needs: engagement, sustainable lifestyles, income, poverty alleviation.
  • Profit model: donations from low-carbon partners, contributions from charitable partners; users pay nothing, which builds loyalty to Alipay and partners.
  • Operating model: organise people, activities and partnerships on Alipay's infrastructure; deliver value through planting and maintenance; owns the platform, not the assets.
  • Partnership model: specialists, low-carbon businesses, charities, nonprofits (China Green Foundation), commercial partners (Hellobike), rural communities.

Class debate: does individual impact matter?

Kanish argued individual actions are tiny and partners fund trees anyway. Answer: aggregation (small changes by 700m daily users) and interdependence: no users, no partner money or attention; no partners, no reason for users to come. That is the cross-side network effect.

Would it work in India?

Candidates named: PhonePe or Paytm (payments base like Alipay), Reliance, Flipkart. Barrier: tribal and local communities often lack property rights (forest pattas) to the trees, berries and hives, so no skin in the game. Fix the rights first. Challenge noted in the case: Ant Forest has not scaled outside China; future plan was 1,000 partners and one billion people.

5B · Scale and scope · formal and informal

What do you aggregate, and who does it?

Scale lives in the capital-heavy middle of the chain. Scope lives at the two ends, where variety is. The informal sector owns the scope-driven collection end in emerging markets.

Economies of scale

More volume through the same asset

  • Fixed costs spread over more input
  • Higher uptime and learning-by-doing
  • Better utilisation of sorters, wash lines and extrusion

Dominates reprocessing (the middle).

Economies of scope

More products or streams from the same capability

  • Shared collection routes and MRFs
  • Shared testing, QA, logistics and sales
  • Co-processing can improve asset utilisation

Dominates collection and compounding/converting (the ends).

Class examples of scope

  • iPhone: one platform personalised by every user
  • Crocs Jibbitz: one shoe, endless add-ons
  • Disney: characters reused across parks, films and merchandise
  • HDFC: one customer base, many products; shared faculty across programmes

Problems of informal systems (class list)

  • Hard to regulate or track; no reliable data for policy
  • Worker exploitation, no wage benchmark, no training
  • Health hazards; no accountability
  • Prices negotiated by power; volatility
  • MNC targets set at home can create perverse incentives locally

In one line

Scope at the ends (bundling many materials per route), scale in the middle (one material through big machines). The switching node is where informal scope meets formal scale.

5D · LCA and value capture · likely calculation

PET: where value and burden coincide

Value capture and life-cycle burden together decide recycling rates. For mono-material plastic (PET, PP) the stage that earns most also pollutes most, so fixing it pays twice.

System boundary (slide 12)

Cradle-to-cradle: collection → sorting (MRF) → reprocessing → compounding/converting, closing the loop back into a new product, which re-enters use and returns to collection.

Excluded: virgin resin production; use phase of the original product; capital equipment manufacturing.

Definitions

SUP here = mono-material single-use plastic (e.g. PET, PP) that can be mechanically recycled once collected and sorted.

Value added at a stage = (price out − price in) − processing cost. LCA burden = stage's % of the chain's footprint. A negative value-capture share means value destroyed.

5E · Multi-layer plastic · Shakti Plastics case

MLP: where value and burden diverge

For laminates the economic problem (collection) and the environmental problem (processing) sit at opposite ends of the chain. Fixing one does not fix the other.

Teal = value capture; orange = LCA burden; red = value destroyed. Value shares sum to 100 (−20+20+70+30).

What this means for MLP (slide 17)

  • MLP = multi-layer / mixed-material laminate packaging (snack pouches, sachets) that mechanical recycling cannot economically separate.
  • Pathway: collection (curbside/aggregator) → sorting & aggregation (bulking, baling) → alternative processing (pyrolysis / cement co-processing) → output & distribution (fuel / feedstock sale).
  • Collection destroys value (−20%): the material is not worth what it costs to gather.
  • Subsidising collection (EPR fees) fixes the value gap but does nothing for the footprint.
  • Alternative processing already captures 70%, so cleaner processing tech (lower-temperature pyrolysis, better catalysts) cuts the most emissions per dollar.

5F · Comparing alternatives · quantitative findings

How many uses before reuse pays off?

A reuse threshold is the number of uses at which a reusable's amortised per-trip impact falls to one single-use item's impact. Weight and reuse rate decide almost everything.

Bags (slide 19, UNEP 2023)

Single-use bag weight: ~6 g in China / India / Singapore / US vs ~19 g in Finland / Spain / UK. A heavier single-use bag lowers the threshold for every alternative.

AlternativeUses to match one SUP bag (climate)
Paper bag4-8
Reusable PE bag5-10
Durable PP bag10-20 (midpoint 15)
Cotton bag50-150 (midpoint 100)

Bio-based / biodegradable bags do not guarantee lower impact; they need reuse too. Starch-based bags showed no benefit over conventional single-use bags in one study.

Left of the crossing the reusable is still worse per trip; right of it reuse has paid off. Cotton tote crosses at 100.

Durable PP bag (footprint = 15 single-use bags)N*=151=1515 uses

Below N*, the reusable's amortised per-trip impact is still higher than a fresh single-use bag; above it, reuse has paid off. Functional unit = one shopping trip.

What a threshold does NOT capture (slide 20)

  1. Measured for climate change (GWP) only: crossover differs for acidification, eutrophication, etc.
  2. Assumes reuse adds no extra impact: true for a dry-goods bag, false for tableware or containers where washing adds impact each cycle.
  3. Says nothing about whether the bag survives 15 trips or the consumer keeps using it.

Functional unit = one shopping trip, not the product lifetime.

6C · Green bonds · market data

A big market that has hit a ceiling

A green bond is a normal bond from the same issuer with ring-fenced use of proceeds. Same credit risk, so it can be compared with a conventional twin.

Green Bond Principles (ICMA)

First green bond: World Bank, 2008; boost after Paris 2015. ICMA wrote the GBP to standardise "green". Three pillars:

  1. Use of proceeds: eligible green projects, new or existing
  2. Management of proceeds: where to park the liquidity
  3. Reporting: impact assessment with a standard green metric

Use cases: renewable energy, energy efficiency, social development, natural resource conservation, green infrastructure.

Greenwashing

Using green bond proceeds for unrelated purposes (mislabelling, unsubstantiated claims, proceeds for brown purposes).

Massachusetts State College Building Authority (2014): green bonds for a parking garage with 2 EV charging points. One green investor called it "pathetic": thin additionality.

6D · Greenium · likely calculation

The greenium is a few basis points

Greenium = yield on comparable conventional bond − yield on green bond. Positive means the issuer borrows more cheaply by going green. Almost everywhere it is small, unstable and shrinking.

Why firms issue, why it is small

Two reasons to issue (S6.2 slide 32): green activities get highlighted; investors with environmental concerns accept a lower yield.

Measured by: twin bonds (same issuer, maturity, coupon); matched same-issuer pairs; spread against issuer curve; dynamic maturity-matched interpolation (Germany, India).

Why small: if green yielded more than its twin, ordinary investors would buy it; eligible projects are plentiful so supply meets ESG demand. A 10bp subsidy is under 1% of principal, which "few will argue" changes an investment decision.

6F · Why issue · Flammer (2021) · greenwashing

The label is a costly signal

Judging a green bond only on coupon savings misses where the value shows up: in the equity, through investor base and signalling, not in the bond's own yield.

Why NOT
  • Why market the bond as "green" at all?
  • Proceeds only for specified projects: why tie your own hands?
  • Reputational risk from greenwashing allegations
  • Greenium (coupon saving) evidence not encouraging
  • More reporting: third-party review, KPIs, annual progress reports
Why YES
  • Green projects get highlighted: ahead in the corporate climate race
  • Signals commitment: tying your hands has advantages
  • Cost saving on the bond is a narrow lens: equity valuation matters
  • Attracts a durable ESG investor base (long-term holders), lower stock volatility

Flammer, "Corporate Green Bonds" (JFE 2021)

  • Stock reaction: +0.49% CAR at announcement (PGP deck rounds to 0.50%); stronger for certified, first-time, and environmentally important sectors (Table: SASB above median 0.699).
  • Environmental performance: ratings up (~+7 points; table 6.118) and CO₂ per $ of assets down ~12.9% (table −10.898) within two years: "green bonds are not merely greenwashing".
  • Ownership: green investors +2.9pp; long-term investors +1.8 to +2.2pp.
  • Greenium: 1.9bp, not significant.
  • Interpretation: a credible signal of commitment, not cheap money.

Repsol

Oil refiner used proceeds to make existing refining more efficient; claimed 1.2 Mt CO₂-eq saved by upgrading heating equipment. Green bond indexes excluded it despite certification: not a drastic change in business model, and it would let Repsol benefit from fossil more than transition.

Royal Schiphol

Amsterdam airport: €500m (2018) and €750m (2020) for energy-efficient airports, EV passenger transport. Footprint flat or growing overall. Green investor: "we do not view this as a green bond. If Schiphol had raised funds to invest in biofuels, we would have viewed it more positively."

Weak incentives

Sustainability-linked bond coupon step-ups are typically ≤25bp vs ~125bp for a rating downgrade (IFC-Amundi): a mismatch that invites greenwashing. Certification is the discriminator, which is where SEBI 2023 points Indian issuers.

6H · Case · Harvard (HBS 9-221-009) · NBIM

Divest, engage or carbon-weight?

Three tools for an investor who wants less carbon: exit (sell), voice (engage as an owner), or tilt (underweight high emitters). A constrained portfolio cannot beat an unconstrained one, so each has a cost.

1 · Divest

Sell fossil holdings

Clean and focused but may punish transitioning producers. Energy stocks −75% vs S&P +50% (mid-2014 to 2019). NBIM ethical exclusions cost ~10bp a year (~130bp over 14 years); on Harvard's $40.9bn that is $765m present value (~$2.5bn lower by 2040).

2 · Engage

Use voice as an owner

NBIM engaged 400 companies in 2019 and pushed Shell on Niger Delta oil spills. Hard to make an oil firm go renewable.

3 · Carbon-weight

Underweight high emitters

Stay invested and back transitions. Measures: total emissions, emissions relative to market cap, intensity (per $ revenue). A 20% cut in carbon intensity moves expected return 8.80% → 8.19% (Exhibit 9).

Harvard

  • Endowment $40.9bn; 71% of students / 67% of faculty favoured divestment; only the 12-member Harvard Corporation decides.
  • HMC stance: "the endowment is fundamentally an economic resource, not a lever to advance political positions"; prefers ESG integration, active ownership, collaboration. Rare past divestments: tobacco, apartheid South Africa.
  • Flip-flop: 2018 abstained on a Chevron decarbonisation report; 2019: operations fossil-fuel neutral by 2026, fossil-free by 2050; signed letter to oil and gas.
  • Divest Harvard: stop new investment, divest existing, reinvest in ESG; faculty and alumni backed it; oil and gas worst sector 2018/19.
  • John Campbell (HBS): moderate view: "reduce the carbon footprint of Harvard's portfolio... increase the cost of capital" for carbon-intensive firms → carbon weighting.
  • Epilogue (Sep 2021): no direct fossil investments; net zero by 2050 across the portfolio.

NBIM (Norway)

  • $1.3tn, ~1.5% of all global equities, 9,000+ holdings. CRO Patrick Du Plessis: "engagement is better than divestment".
  • Pillar 1: best-practice frameworks; expectations documents.
  • Pillar 2 (engagement): push disclosure, publicly rate firms, "words to numbers".
  • Pillar 3 (exclusions): ethical (190 firms: human rights, tobacco, coal) and risk-based (transition hard; future regulation). Exclusion cost: ~10bp a year (S6.1) or 1.1% cumulative (S6.2).
  • Notable exclusions: Adani Ports & SEZ (rights in war), Airbus and Boeing (nuclear weapons), Bombardier (gross corruption), Coal India, NTPC (coal), POSCO and Vedanta (environmental damage), Zuari Agro (human rights).
  • Re-inclusion: Drax (UK) moved from coal to biomass; exclusion arguably spurred the transition.

6J · Banks · Shell · Citi · Just Climate · close

Climate risk, banks and what finance changes

For a bank, net zero means financed emissions (its clients), not its own offices. Each case asks what climate finance actually changes.

Physical risk

Loan performance deteriorates due to climate events and disasters; amplified because at-risk insurance is now very expensive or unavailable.

Transition risk

Businesses funded by banks fail to transition and lose business and profits, or the transition is very expensive for them.

Developments for banks (S6.2 slides 63-65)

  • NZBA (2023): 138 of the largest banks, 44% of banking loans (BofA, Goldman Sachs, Citi), pledged net-zero portfolios. (S6.1 Citi slide: 114 banks, 41 countries, ~40% of assets, July 2022.)
  • Citi announced net zero in 2021; issues green bonds and helps clients move away from fossil fuels. Firms without transition plans risk losing bank capital.
  • RBI Green Deposits (2023) for banks and NBFCs. BIS recommends disclosure of physical and transition risk; RBI mandated disclosure of climate-risk governance, management and targets from Feb 2024.

Shell (IESE IES912)

Split green and brown, or stay integrated?

  • Integrated gas (LNG, renewables): 18% of revenue, 44% of net income (2021). Upstream 14% / 40%; downstream 68% / 29%; corporate −14%.
  • Third Point (Dan Loeb, ~$17bn AUM, stake >$750m) wants LNG, renewables and marketing spun off.
  • Shell at a 35% discount to Exxon, Chevron; EV/EBITDA 4.45 vs Exxon 7.34, Chevron 6.14, Total 5.65, BP 4.78.
  • Plan: product carbon intensity −20% by 2030, −45% by 2035 (vs 2016), 89% vote; Dutch court ordered −45% by 2030 vs 2019.

Citi (Darden UV8587)

What does net zero mean for a bank?

  • Pledges (2021): net zero 2050; $1tn sustainable finance by 2030, half environmental.
  • $222.1bn in 2020-21: debt capital markets 108.0, sustainable M&A 62.7, muni underwriting 22.8, corporate lending 20.0, ECM 6.2: ~90% investment-banking facilitation.

Just Climate (Haas B6047)

Can manager pay be tied to tonnes abated?

  • 100% of carry scaled by Impact Factor = actual ÷ target abatement over 10 years (cap 100%). Hit 70% → keep 70% of carry.
  • Hard-to-abate sectors (steel, concrete, heating, transport): 50% of emissions, 10% of investment; "too big for VC, too asset-heavy for growth, too nascent for infrastructure".

7A · Session 7 · Prof. Apoorv Gogar · Accounting

Place the vocabulary

Sustainability is the destination. CSR is what a firm does for society. ESG is how outsiders measure and price it.

Six learning objectives (slides 2 and 34)

  1. Place the vocabulary: distinguish CSR, ESG and sustainability; where ESG came from.
  2. Measure E and S: how emissions and social performance are quantified, where numbers are soft.
  3. Understand governance: why weak G turns E and S disclosure into cheap talk.
  4. Read the compliance architecture: CSR mandate, BRSR, BRSR Core vs EU and ISSB.
  5. Use ESG ratings critically: what raters add, why they disagree, how they are regulated.
  6. Spot greenwashing: using the disclosure, the assurance report and the rating.
Friedman (1970): the shareholder
  • Managers are agents; they work for the owners.
  • Spending owners' money on causes is a tax nobody voted for.
  • One duty: profit within the rules; stay legal, compete honestly.
  • Society gains through markets and law, not corporate goodwill.
Freeman (1984): the stakeholders
  • The firm owes duties to everyone it affects: employees, customers, communities, environment.
  • Shareholders are one claimant among several.
  • Profit is the outcome, not the purpose.
  • Managing duties is strategy, not charity: loyalty, reputation, resilience.

Same question, two answers: is CSR shareholders' money given away, or an investment that pays back?

What is ESG? (slide 5)

A framework for assessing a firm on dimensions financial statements do not capture directly.

  • E: how the firm draws on and affects natural systems: emissions, energy, water, waste, physical and transition climate risks.
  • S: how it treats people: employees, safety, supply chain, customers, communities.
  • G: integrity of decision-making: who holds decision rights, how monitored, how performance is disclosed.

Where the term came from

  • Who Cares Wins (2004), under the UN Global Compact with the Swiss government and financial institutions.
  • Material, not merely ethical: lets institutions treat ESG as part of their duty to returns, not charity.
  • From exclusion to integration: SRI screened out tobacco and arms; ESG asks not what to avoid owning but what to price.

7B · Measuring E

An emissions figure is an estimate

Activity data × emission factor = tonnes of CO₂e (global warming potentials convert each gas to CO₂e). Every E disclosure is a quantity, a normaliser and a boundary.

Emissions

Scope 1 and 2 GHG in tCO₂e, plus intensity. Other air emissions (NOx, SOx, particulates). Scope 3 voluntary.

Energy

Total consumption in joules, renewable and non-renewable split, intensity per rupee of turnover.

Water

Withdrawal by source, consumption, discharge by destination and treatment, use in water-stressed areas.

Waste & circularity

Generated by type, hazardous and non-hazardous, how much recycled, reused or diverted.

Why E standardised first

  • Physical units: a tonne of CO₂e or a kilolitre means the same everywhere.
  • Established protocols: the GHG Protocol since 2001, long before regulators.
  • Auditable trails: fuel bills, electricity meters, purchase records already exist.

All four sit in BRSR Core, so each carries an intensity ratio per rupee of turnover and comes under assessment or assurance for the largest listed firms.

Scope 1 · direct

Fuel burnt in owned or controlled sources: boilers, furnaces, company vehicles; process and fugitive emissions.

BRSR essential indicator

Scope 2 · purchased energy

Electricity, steam, heat, cooling bought in. Two conventions: location-based (grid average) and market-based (contracts, certificates).

BRSR essential indicator

Scope 3 · value chain

Fifteen categories up and downstream: purchased goods, transport, use of sold products, investments. Usually the biggest, least controllable.

BRSR leadership indicator (voluntary)

Choices that move the number

  • Boundary: equity share or operational control
  • Absolute tonnes or intensity per rupee of turnover
  • Which emission factors, and from which year

Why the accountant is needed

  • An emissions figure is an estimate, not a measurement
  • Scope 3 rests on supplier data the firm does not control
  • Restatements are common; comparability suffers
Whose chimney? Your own = Scope 1. Someone else's Scope 1 that you buy as energy = your Scope 2. Everything else in the value chain = Scope 3. Timberland estimated over 95% of its footprint is Scope 3 (Pucker).

7E · Governance

Weak G turns E and S into cheap talk

Governance is not one third of ESG. It is the part that decides whether the other two thirds mean anything.

Why governance exists

  • Berle & Means (1932): ownership separated from control. Owners supply capital; managers decide.
  • Jensen & Meckling (1976): agency costs = value lost to self-interest + monitoring cost + bonding cost. Reduced, never eliminated.
  • Governance is the set of arrangements that keeps agency costs in check so delegation is worth its price.

Two conflicts, not one

Owner vs manager

Dispersed shareholders cannot coordinate; management entrenches. Classic US/UK problem.

Controlling vs minority

A promoter with a controlling block extracts private benefits from minority holders. Classic Indian problem.

Internal: a board that works
  • Majority independent, tested against the promoter, not only management
  • Chair separate from CEO; fully independent audit, nomination, risk committees
  • Genuine financial expertise, not formal qualification alone
  • Executive sessions without management; directors nominated by the board, not the promoter
  • Attendance, recorded dissent, willingness to resign with a stated reason
  • Tools: statutory and internal audit, internal financial controls, prior approval of every RPT, pay with deferral and clawback, appointment and succession, disclosure, vigil mechanism to the audit committee chair
External: when the board does not bind
  • Audit and regulation: statutory audit, mandatory auditor rotation, NFRA, SEBI LODR
  • Institutional investors: voting, engagement; SEBI Stewardship Code (2020) requires disclosure of voting policy and votes
  • Proxy advisors: IiAS, SES, InGovern
  • Analysts, press, short sellers (coverage tilts to buy and hold)
  • Minority protection: majority-of-minority approval for material RPTs (SEBI, 2022)
  • Market for corporate control, creditors, courts: takeovers, IBC, class action under Section 245

Where this leaves us (slide 15)

  1. The apparatus existed in every case. Boards met, audit committees sat, auditors signed. Structure did not bind.
  2. Independence is tested against the wrong person: rules test against management; in India the conflict is with the promoter.
  3. Look at conduct, not structure: attendance and recorded dissent, real expertise, arm's-length RPTs, auditor resignations, promoter pledging.
  4. BRSR quality is itself a governance outcome.

These failures rarely occur alone: one conflict usually funds another.

7F · Regulation · CSR mandate · likely calculation

Spend 2%, or transfer, or pay

India's pattern: disclose, spend, standardise, verify, regulate the intermediaries, then recalibrate for cost (2025).

Section 135, Companies Act 2013

  • Applies if, in the preceding year, any of: net worth ≥ ₹500 crore; turnover ≥ ₹1,000 crore; net profit ≥ ₹5 crore (Corporate Laws (Amendment) Bill 2026 proposes ₹10 crore; JPC reported Aug 2026; not yet passed).
  • Spend 2% of average net profit of the last three financial years, every year.
  • Governance: board CSR committee, board-approved policy, annual action plan; implementing agencies must be registered.
  • What counts: Schedule VII causes (health, education, hunger and poverty, environment, rural development, sports). Not eligible: staff benefits, political gifts, ordinary business.
  • Unspent: ongoing projects → Unspent CSR Account within 30 days (Bill proposes 90), spend within 3 years; other unspent → specified fund within 6 months.
  • Enforcement: penalty up to 2x the shortfall (company capped at ₹1 crore); officers up to ₹2 lakh. Board report and Form CSR-2. Independent impact assessment if average obligation ≥ ₹10 crore.
  • Hardened from "spend or explain" (2013) to spend, transfer or be penalised (2019, 2021 amendments).

Manchiraju & Rajgopal (2017, JAR 55(5)): Companies Act thresholds as a natural experiment. Negative valuation effects for firms forced to spend, unlike the positive link for voluntary CSR.

Crowding out

Rajgopal & Tantri (2023, JAR 61(1)): former voluntary givers cut spending afterwards: the 2% floor becomes the target. Signalling value lost, advertising rises, stock price and operating performance decline.

Real outcomes

Anantharaman, Manchiraju & Misra (2026, RAST 31(2)): vs matched global peers, better E and S ratings (community engagement, resource stewardship), concentrated where spending rose a lot and committees were independent and expert.

Reconciling them

The mandate raises the floor for firms that gave nothing and lowers the ceiling for firms that gave freely. Implementation quality decides which effect dominates.

7G · BRSR · BRSR Core · assurance

Read the boundary before the number

BRSR is prescribed, not materiality-led: firms report what is asked. Core adds verification on nine attributes. The level of comfort is in the assurance signature.

BRSR basics (slide 21)

  • Who: top 1,000 listed by market cap; mandatory FY2022-23, voluntary FY2021-22.
  • Where: inside the annual report under SEBI LODR Reg. 34(2)(f).
  • Replaced the narrative BRR (since 2012). Built on the nine NGRBC principles (2019).
  • Section A: general disclosures (profile, operations, workforce, CSR, material issues). B: management and process (policies, board oversight per principle). C: principle-wise performance: essential indicators (all) and leadership indicators (voluntary).

The nine NGRBC principles

  1. Ethics and transparency
  2. Sustainable products
  3. Employee well-being
  4. Stakeholder engagement
  5. Human rights
  6. Environment (P6: the E numbers)
  7. Policy advocacy
  8. Inclusive growth
  9. Consumer value

BRSR Core: nine attributes (slide 22)

Environmental: GHG footprint, water footprint, energy footprint, circularity (waste). Social: employee well-being and safety, gender diversity, inclusive development. Governance: fairness to customers and suppliers, openness of business.

9A · Session 9 · Prof. Ram Nidumolu · OB

Freedom in a commons brings ruin to all

Hardin (1968): when a resource is open to all, each user's gain is private and the cost is shared, so rational individuals deplete what they all depend on.

Pollution: the reverse form (slide 3)

"Not a question of taking something out of the commons, but of putting something in: sewage, or chemical, radioactive and heat wastes into water; noxious and dangerous fumes into the air; and distracting and unpleasant advertising signs into the line of sight. The rational man finds that his share of the cost of the wastes he discharges into the commons is less than the cost of purifying his wastes... we are locked into a system of 'fouling our own nest'." Section heading: "How to legislate temperance?" (pollution as a function of population density).

Hardin's remedies, and the class list

  • No technical solution: needs a change in values or institutions; "mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon". Appeals to conscience fail (the conscientious lose out). His central example was population growth.
  • Class tools: limits or caps; taxes and fees (Singapore congestion charges); cap with tradable credits; property rights; conscience and social shame (weakest).
  • Global commons are hardest: no one can coerce nations. Rare successes: Montreal Protocol (CFCs), nuclear arms limitation (NPT, SALT). Paris was voluntary. Club of Rome warned in 1972. AI may follow the same path.
Professor's own sample MCQ (S10 slide 3): "In the Tragedy of the Commons, what is NOT an enabling condition?" (a) act out of self-interest (b) believe their contribution is small (c) seek to take control of the commons (d) not thinking of aggregate impacts. Answer: (c). Users are not trying to seize it; they just pursue their livelihood and ignore the aggregate.

Stakeholders of a firm (slide 6)

"Individuals and constituencies that contribute, either voluntarily or involuntarily, to its wealth-creating capacity and activities, and who are therefore its potential beneficiaries and/or risk bearers." (Post, Preston & Sachs, CMR 2002)

Stakeholders are those who: 1) supply critical resources, and/or 2) place something of value "at risk", and/or 3) have sufficient power to affect performance.

Three groups (slide 7)

  1. Resource base: investors (share/bond holders), employees, customers/users
  2. Industry structure: supply/demand chain partners, JV partners and alliances, regulators, unions
  3. Sociopolitical: governments, communities/citizens, private organisations, environment

Class debate

Resolution: interdependence. My success depends on stakeholders' success; employees too have skin in the game (livelihood, switching costs). Stakeholder capitalism is the older model in India: Jamsetji Tata's Empress Mills offered creches, pensions and leave in the 19th century.

Legal licence, lost social licence

Groundwater is an open-access commons with no legal limit in Rajasthan. Coca-Cola had every permit. The question is whether it met its own CSR standard and kept the community's consent.

Facts against Coca-Cola
  • Plant set up 1999 (production 2000) in a RIICO park, a year after the overexploited designation; four 100 m bore wells (permission for five).
  • Claims environmental due diligence but will not share the reports.
  • Rajasthan gave a sales-tax incentive equal to the investment, ₹39 crore (~$9.1m), which may explain a plant running at ~30% capacity.
  • Peak production April-June: the most acute water stress.
  • TERI (the auditor Coke chose) said the plant would remain a contributor to a worsening situation; four options: transport water from an unstressed aquifer, store water from low-stress seasons, relocate, shut down. Coke rejected all four.

Facts for Coca-Cola (or mitigating)
  • Agriculture is ~91-95% of extraction; farm use alone exceeds recharge.
  • Causes: population growth (~2.6%/yr), urbanisation, water-intensive crops (groundnut, mustard, wheat, millet), free or cheap electricity for farm pumps.
  • Rainfall decline is not the cause (avg ~600 mm, sd ~175 mm).
  • Water-use ratio fell from ~2.7 to ~2.12 litres per litre of product (glass to PET); extraction roughly halved.
  • All legal clearances held; Rajasthan has no groundwater law; State Water Policy (1999) priorities (drinking, irrigation, power, industry) never enacted.
  • Location decision predates Coke's global water-stewardship push (first environmental report 2002; Seabright appointed 2003).

10B · Three schools · Van Staveren (2007)

Ends, duties, or character

Utilitarianism judges consequences. Deontology judges duties and motives. Virtue ethics judges the character of a good person acting in context, the intermediate position.

Utilitarianism (Mill) · slide 8

Actions are morally acceptable based on their consequences for the happiness, satisfaction, welfare or utility of those affected. "An improvable science of behaviour."

  1. Aligned with naturalistic approaches to social sciences
  2. Initially cardinal and interpersonal utility
  3. Later Pareto: raise one agent's utility without reducing others'
  4. Basis of the rational utility function of economics
  5. Measures consequences: consequentialism; emphasises prediction
  6. Critique: if beliefs, desires, actions are predictable, what does it mean to be an autonomous agent with free will?
Kantian deontology · slide 9
  1. Humans have special duties and rights that take precedence over policies that violate rights for the greater good
  2. Rights and duties arise from pure reason, not experience

Categorical imperative: (1) endorse only principles all others would endorse; (2) treat moral agents as ends, not means: the opposite of utilitarianism.

  1. Autonomy matters more than costs and benefits
  2. Morality focuses on motives, not consequences
  3. Free will drives beliefs, desires, actions (not causal); a priori knowledge is possible

"The starry heavens above me and the moral law within me."

Virtue (areteological) ethics · slide 10

Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics): focuses on the virtues of a "good person".

  1. Virtues: justice, generosity, liberality, kindness
  2. Contextual responsibility in instances
  3. Mahabharata's contextual virtue ethics
  4. Intermediate position between the ends of utilitarianism and motives of deontology: (a) reconciles self-interest with altruism (b) the good has no universal standard
  5. Moral behaviour is imperfect and continuously adapting

"Virtue ethics is expressed in relationships; morality is concerned with concrete social life instead of abstract moral rules" (Van Staveren). Limitations: meant for the individual, not society; good intentions could lead to bad outcomes.

Experiment: $3→$9 directly judged worse than selling to Y who charges $15When handing off or outsourcing work, ask whether the assignment might invite unethical behaviour; take ownership
The slippery slopeWe are less able to see unethical behaviour when it develops graduallyAuditors more likely to accept a client's questionable statements if infractions accrue over timeJar experiment: auditors twice as likely to approve inflated estimates reached gradually (boiling frog)Be alert for even trivial infractions and address them immediately; check whether behaviour has changed
Overvaluing outcomesWe give a pass to unethical behaviour if the outcome is goodA researcher whose fraudulent clinical trial saves lives is considered more ethical than one whose trial leads to deathsLaw punishes the brother who kills by accident more than the one who tries and missesExamine both "good" and "bad" decisions; reward sound processes, not just good outcomes

Ethical fading (Tenbrunsel & Messick)

Framing a choice as purely a business decision removes its moral dimension. Ford Pinto: a cost-benefit analysis found it cheaper to pay lawsuits than fix fuel tanks. Lee Iacocca: "Read the product objectives and get back to work."

Managerial challenge

Firms spend ~$1m per $1bn revenue on compliance, yet observed unethical behaviour rises. Don't "force" ethics through surveillance alone: teach the biases; ask "What ethical implications might arise from this decision?"; know your own blind spots.

Spot the breakdown fast: incentive/target → ill-conceived goals · "not my business to look" → motivated blindness · outsourced / third party → indirect blindness · gradual / small steps → slippery slope · "but it worked" → overvaluing outcomes.

Slippery slope

A plant manager under-reports emissions by 1% one quarter, 3% the next, 10% a year later.

A team fudges a pilot's data; the product succeeds, so leadership never asks how the pilot passed.
