Session 1 · Module A

Scarcity, ethics and fairness: why we even argue

Before any maths, the class set up the philosophical problem: we have scarce resources, and every choice about the future is a choice about who gets what.

Does abundance end conflict?

The class opened with the big idea that life is full of trade-offs because resources are limited (even the richest person has only 24 hours). The professor told of asking the Dalai Lama whether more resources would make humanity kinder. The answer was No: "humans, unless they change the way they think... will always create scarcity, even when there isn't any. No matter how much you have, you always want more." We adapt to each new level of plenty and want more (whatever Elon Musk says about AI abundance). Wars in Ukraine and the Middle East show we are not fighting less than a century ago. So abundance alone cannot solve conflict. Under scarcity we need human judgment, and that judgment builds two systems:

Ethicists

Reason from moral first principles. Prioritise qualitative values, are suspicious of letting "hard numbers" decide how much future people matter. Core idea: intergenerational neutrality (a person born in 2250 counts as much as one born in 2026).

vs

Positivists

Rely on data and what markets actually reveal. Use market interest rates and optimisation models to allocate resources. Core idea: opportunity cost (never fund a project that earns less than the market).

Two instincts: selfish survival and benevolence

  • Survival (the "selfish gene"). The primal instinct is to protect yourself, your kin and your tribe. It drives much of our behaviour.
  • Benevolence and empathy. The class debated where caring for others comes from: group survival, reciprocity ("I help you, you help me later", which the professor called an exchange rather than deep benevolence), division of labour (Adam Smith), the stories we tell ourselves (Harari). The professor's answer: empathy comes from uncertainty. "Bad things can happen to anyone", so you can picture yourself in the other person's place. That is exactly Rawls' insight.

Exchange: humanity's core institution for handling uncertainty

  • Animals cooperate, but only humans can write contracts and build trust across long periods (language, writing, stories). That lets us move resources across time and across people.
  • Insurance has no reciprocity: 50 car owners pool premiums and whoever crashes gets paid. Markets take insurance beyond the tribe and village to strangers across cities and countries. "Finance is all about risk sharing."
  • Some argue trade and cross-border investment even reduce war: you are less likely to bomb a country you have a stake in.
  • Rawls and the veil of ignorance (A Theory of Justice). Rawls argued democracy is not enough: majority rule does not protect minorities. Design the system as if you did not know whether you would be Hindu or Muslim, man or woman, rich or poor, and you choose justice, because you could be the minority. The sense of fairness is so strong that people reject a better outcome if it feels unjust (you will see this in negotiation class). John Rawls asked: what social contract would you design if you did not know who you would be (rich or poor, which race, gender, or generation)? Behind this veil you would pick a system that protects the most vulnerable. Applied across time, you would not accept a rule that lets the present enrich itself while dumping catastrophe on the future, because you might be born into that future.
Reading link

Weisbach & Sunstein use exactly this: "Under a deontological approach with a Rawlsian foundation, the generation in which one finds oneself is irrelevant from the moral point of view." Rawls also has a just savings principle: each generation should save a fair amount for the next.

Remember
  • Dalai Lama: we create scarcity even amid plenty. Empathy comes from uncertainty. Exchange moves resources across time (lending) and across people (insurance).
  • Sustainability = constraint ("what I leave is what I cannot have"). Ethics = judgment ("how much should I leave").
  • Ethicists reason from principles (neutrality); positivists read the market (opportunity cost).
  • Rawls' veil of ignorance is the fairness test for intergenerational choices.

Session 1 · Module B

Utility, compounding and the Ramsey equation

This is the maths engine of Session 1. Three building blocks: diminishing marginal utility, compounding, and the Ramsey formula that combines them.

Block 1: Utility with diminishing returns

Economists model wellbeing as a utility function U(C) of consumption C. Two properties:

  • U'(C) > 0: more consumption is always better.
  • U''(C) < 0: each extra rupee adds less happiness than the one before (diminishing marginal utility). ₹1 lakh changes a poor family's life; it barely registers for a billionaire.

The class used the standard isoelastic (constant relative risk aversion) form:

U(C) = C1−η / (1 − η)if η = 1, this becomes U(C) = ln(C)

η (eta) is the inequality aversion (equity) parameter: how fast marginal utility falls as people get richer. Higher η = you care more about giving to the poorer party.

  • η = 0: U = C, a straight line. "Doesn't give a damn about equity."
  • η = 1: U = ln C (Stern's choice).
  • η = 2: U = −1/C, even more curved (Nordhaus's choice). Negative utility is fine: only the ordering matters.

Ordinal vs cardinal: ordinal means only the order matters (higher C is better); cardinal means the actual numbers matter. In class the professor said utility functions here are treated as ordinal: "only the order matters, not the actual number". (Nuance for a strong answer: adding utilities across generations, as Ramsey does, strictly leans on cardinal comparisons.)

Two class thought experiments that build the ethics into the maths

1. The three families (why η exists)

Family 1 earns ₹50 lakh a year, family 2 ₹5 crore, family 3 ₹50 crore. Whom would you help? Almost everyone picks the poorest: money means less to the rich. Now the twist: these are your descendants. We are 10 to 20 times richer than people 100 years ago; our great-grandchildren may be far richer than us. So by the same intuition, why sacrifice much for them? That intuition is η.

2. Your child vs your brother's child (why δ exists)

Most people help their own child first. Biology: you share half your genes with your child, and far less with a niece or nephew. Each generation halves the link, so 100 years is about 6 generations, roughly 1/64 of your genes: "they look nothing like you". The positivist (Nordhaus-style) point: these people are genetically distant, and today's poor also need hospitals and schools. Stern's reply: all generations are equal. That tension is δ.

The sustainability challenge is to widen the circle of concern beyond our own genetic line, to strangers living centuries away. And a reminder the professor repeated: "there's no such thing as a utility function. It's just mathematics", a disciplined way of saying in symbols what we believe about people.

Block 2: Compounding and the Rule of 70

X·ert = 2X ⇒ rt = ln 2 ≈ 0.7 ⇒ doubling time T ≈ 70 / r(%)At 7% money doubles in about 10 years; at 1.4% in 50 years; at 5.5% in about 13 years.
Exam alert from class

The professor said of the Rule of 70: "I'm going to ask this question in the midterm." He also wants you to do these calculations in your head, without a calculator or AI. Practise: 7% → 10 yrs; 10% → 7 yrs; 14% → 5 yrs; 1.4% → 50 yrs (so ×4 in 100 years); 5.5% → ~13 yrs (so about 8 doublings, ×256 in 100 years).

Over a decade, small differences in rates are negligible. Over centuries they become gigantic, which is why the Stern vs Nordhaus gap of about 4 percentage points turns into a factor of nearly 2,800.

Block 3: The Ramsey problem and equation

Frank Ramsey (1928) imagined a social planner choosing consumption for every generation to maximise the discounted sum of their utilities:

Max [ U(C0) + U(C1)/(1+δ) + U(C2)/(1+δ)2 + ... ]δ (delta) = pure rate of time preference: how much less a future person's utility counts just because they live later
In class: the two-period derivation (know the steps)
  1. Maximise U(C0) + U(C1)/(1+δ) subject to the planet's wealth W = C0 + C1/(1+r): whatever we don't consume today is invested and grows at r.
  2. First-order (Euler) condition: U'(C0) / U'(C1) = (1+r)/(1+δ).
  3. With log utility (η = 1), U'(C) = 1/C, so C1/C0 = (1+r)/(1+δ). And C1/C0 = 1+g.
  4. So 1+r = (1+δ)(1+g) = 1 + δ + g + δg. The cross term δg is tiny (0.01 × 0.02 = 0.0002), so r ≈ δ + g.
  5. For general η: r = δ + ηg. If δ = 0 and η = 0, r = 0: the extreme ethicist position, which "even the extreme ethicists don't like".

Solving this gives the Ramsey equation for the social discount rate:

r = δ + η·g
Why the formula makes sense (say this in the exam)

We discount the future for two separate reasons. (1) Impatience: we may simply weight later people less (δ). (2) They will be richer: if consumption grows at g, a rupee is worth less to them, and η converts that growth into a utility discount. Setting δ = 0 does not mean r = 0. Even a perfectly impartial planner discounts because the future is richer. The ethicists say this second part is "not really discounting, it is distributive preference that only resembles discounting by coincidence".

The egalitarian paradox (a favourite trick question)

A higher η (more egalitarian) gives a higher discount rate and so less climate spending, because the future is expected to be richer and egalitarians want to transfer toward the poorer party, which is us today. Weisbach & Sunstein: "strong egalitarians should care less about the future". Nordhaus uses η = 2 (more egalitarian) than Stern's η = 1.

Real vs nominal rates. Long-run models work in real terms using the Fisher equation (1 + R) / (1 + π) = 1 + r, where R is the nominal rate and π inflation. Inflation cancels out, so you can ignore it in these problems.

Remember
  • U'(C) > 0, U''(C) < 0. η = inequality aversion; η = 1 gives ln C.
  • Rule of 70: doubling time = 70 / rate.
  • r = δ + ηg. δ and η are ethics; g is an estimate.

Number check: Nordhaus's g

In class the professor said Nordhaus uses long-run world growth of about 2.3%, but 1.5 + 2×2.3 = 6.1%, not 5.5%. The Weisbach & Sunstein reading uses g = 2%, which is what makes 5.5% add up. In the exam write 1.5 + 2×2 = 5.5% and, if you like, add that historic world growth has been about 2.3%.

In class: the $100 trillion worked example (do it in your head)

Suppose climate damage will cost future generations $100 trillion in 100 years (for scale, the professor put world GDP at roughly $60 to 70 trillion). How much is it worth spending today to prevent it? Take the present value.

  • Stern, 1.4%: money doubles every 70/1.4 = 50 years, so twice in 100 years: ×4. PV = 100/4 = $25 trillion. Spend up to a quarter of the damage today.
  • Nordhaus, 5.5%: doubles every ~13 years, so about 8 doublings in 100 years: 28 = ×256. PV = 100/256 ≈ $0.4 trillion (about $390 billion). "Not even a trillion."
  • You might expect a rate about four times bigger to change the answer about four times. Instead it is 4× vs 256×, a 64-fold gap with the Rule of 70 shortcut (the exact calculation gives 4.0× vs 211×, about 53-fold).

The transcript has the professor saying "$400 million" at this point, but 100 ÷ 256 trillion is about $390 billion, which matches his "not even a trillion". Use billion.

In class: labels mislead, models clarify

Nordhaus, the "positivist", uses a higher η (2) than Stern (1), so on equity he looks more ethical. "This is the reason we need to write down these models, because these terms are otherwise very vague." The disagreement is not whether to act on climate but how much to spend today, and that reduces to δ, η and g. Subjectivity is everywhere: valuing a company also splits into cash flows, a discount rate and a growth rate, and you ask whether each piece is reasonable. Stern's small δ = 0.1% is the yearly chance that humanity wipes itself out (nuclear war, a meteor): no point saving for people who will not exist.

Session 1 · Module D · Reading 1

Weisbach & Sunstein: "they are answering different questions"

"Climate Change and Discounting the Future: A Guide for the Perplexed" (Yale Law & Policy Review, 2009). The single most important reading for Session 1. Its punchline: ethicists and positivists are talking past each other.

Why discount at all? The bank account logic

You have $100. A project pays $110 in two years. A bank pays 6%. The bank turns $100 into $112.36, so the project loses. Equivalently, PV of $110 at 6% = 110/1.062 = $97.90 < $100. At a 4% bank rate, PV = $101.70, so the project wins. Discounting is just a way to account for opportunity cost. It only makes sense because an alternative (the bank) exists. Robinson Crusoe, alone on an island with no bank, has zero opportunity cost; his choice is purely about what he wants when. The ethicists implicitly say climate is a Robinson Crusoe world; the positivists say we do have "the bank".

So funding the low-return project wastes either $98 bn today or $20 tn in 100 years. Everyone could be better off: invest $5 bn at the market and future generations get about $1 tn, while today keeps $95 bn more. Positivists insist they are discounting money, not lives: if a statistical life is worth $5 million today and in 200 years, you only need to set aside the present value of $5 million now, because money grows.

Three complications the positivists must handle

  1. Private vs social returns: taxes create a wedge between what investors earn (after tax) and what society earns (pre-tax, including tax revenue). There are also many "market rates" (T-bills, corporate bonds, equities). Technical, but it matters.
  2. Uncertainty: if the rate is 10% or 2% with equal odds, the right rate is not 6% but about 3.4%. You average present values, not rates, and the low-rate scenario dominates. So "the discount rate used by the positivists should be near the very lowest expected rate of return over the long run" (Weitzman's "lowest possible rate"). Climate may itself lower future returns, pushing the rate lower still. (Worked in Module E.)
  3. The richer future: richer people value the environment more, and climate damage makes environmental goods scarcer. So future environmental benefits should get a higher value.

The ethicist case: three arguments

1. Private ≠ social return

Unless markets are perfect, private returns understate social returns. Individuals may demand 5.5% but society could benefit even at 1.4%. Using the market rate would reject worthwhile projects.

2. Individuals neglect posterity

Market rates come from people saving for their own lifetime and maybe their grandkids. Nobody is thinking 300 years ahead, so market rates answer the wrong question.

3. Rates are endogenous

Fighting climate change reshapes the whole economy, so the rate of return is something we choose, not observe. Choosing it means going back to ethics, which leads to the Ramsey formula.

The "Florida" example: if sea-level rise destroys Florida in 200 years and abatement is costly, discounted cost-benefit analysis might say "do little". That says nothing about whether we are treating descendants justly. Under Rawlsian or welfarist ethics we may owe them prevention or compensation.

W&S's jab at the ethicists

The Ramsey formula needs two "very specialized assumptions" (a planner maximising summed welfare, and the isoelastic utility form chosen "largely for mathematical convenience"). W&S call this "simply astonishing" given that Stern attacks positivists for relying on special assumptions.

The central claim: separate the two questions

  1. Suppose our legacy to the future is the ethically right amount, say $100. A new project costs $10 today and gives the future $20. We can do it and trim the legacy elsewhere, still leaving $100. The only question is whether this $10 earns more than $10 elsewhere. That is opportunity cost = discounting at the market rate.
  2. Now we learn climate change means the legacy is really only $70. Ethics says we should raise it (to, say, $95, since all generations share the loss).
  3. How to raise it? Cheapest way first. If one project costs $10 and leaves $25, do not pick one that costs more for the same result. The market rate still measures opportunity cost.
  4. Conclusion: ethics sets the size of the legacy; the market rate picks the projects. If the ethicists are right that the rate should be 1.4%, the real implication is that we should be saving vastly more overall, not funding low-return climate projects first.

Both sides make a mistake. Ethicists should argue for higher savings and investment, not lower discount rates. Positivists must not use discounting as an excuse to abandon the future: a Nordhaus-style modest abatement may need to be paired with other projects that protect future welfare. Climate abatement beyond Nordhaus is justified "if and only if" it is among the highest-return ways to help the future.

  • Ethicists = distribution (how much to leave). Positivists = efficiency (which projects). Separable.
  • If CBA gives an indefensible answer, fix the savings plan, not the discount rate.

Session 1 · Module E

Uncertainty, who pays, the petrol tax, and three paradoxes

The last third of the lecture moved from theory to the real world: uncertain growth, why the consensus discount rate fell to 2%, India vs Europe, and what a 2% rate would do to the price of petrol.

Averaging under uncertainty: why the low rate wins

World growth is estimated at about 2.3 to 2.4%. Suppose 50% chance growth is 2% and 50% chance it is 3%. You might discount at 2.5%. Wrong. You must average the present values:

Expected PV = ½·C/(1.02)100 + ½·C/(1.03)100 = C × (0.5×0.138 + 0.5×0.052) = 0.095CThe single rate giving 0.095 over 100 years is about 2.38%, below the 2.5% average

Because PV is convex in the rate, the bad (low-growth, low-rate) world dominates: "you don't take the average of the discount rate, you take the average of the present values... it's in the denominator." The longer the horizon, the stronger the pull: over 200 years the right rate is even closer to 2%, over 500 years closer still. Lesson: for the very long term, use the smallest plausible rate; otherwise you short-change future generations. W&S's version: 10% or 2% over 50 years gives PVs of about $8,500 and $372,000 per $1 million; average about $190,000; implied rate about 3.4%, not 6%.

Why the consensus rate fell from 3% to about 2%

Stern was criticised and revised his 1.4% upward; Nordhaus came well below 5.5%. The world consensus, once about 3%, is now about 2%. Three reasons from class:

1. Taxes

Historic real returns of 2 to 3% are partly a transfer to the government as tax ("I may be earning 3%, actually it's only 2"). The professor argued the rate to use is the tax-adjusted version, which is smaller.

2. Growth is not given

A student's point: growth depends on what we do. If we do not save the planet we will not grow as much, so future growth numbers should be more modest than history (the circularity problem below).

3. Uncertainty

The historic 2.3% average includes the Industrial Revolution, which may not repeat. If unsure between 2% and 3%, average PVs: the answer sits near 2%.

Who should abate and who should pay? India vs Europe

The Ramsey formula explains the negotiating table. Different countries disagree on g, so they disagree on r. India grows fast, so its r is higher and each rupee spent on abatement today hurts more. Europe grows slowly, has a lower r, and wants carbon taxes and green spending. In the professor's telling of India's position (External Affairs Minister Jaishankar): we are still growing and need hospitals, growth and productivity; you grew for 100 years when nobody worried about climate; you did the damage, so if you want us to abate, subsidise us. Europe's answer: go green and we will subsidise you, for example with carbon credits. "All these philosophical discussions ultimately boil down to these numbers": every Paris-style negotiation is a negotiation over δ, η and g. (A student raised colonialism as a further reason for compensation; the professor called that a different argument from the discounting one.)

Reading link: Mankiw, "One answer to global warming: a new tax" (NYT, 2007)

Mankiw argues most economists favour a carbon tax (the "Pigou Club"): it is the most efficient tool, it raises revenue that can cut other taxes or fund rebates, and it gives a clear price signal. He points out politicians prefer cap and trade partly because it hides the cost (free permits are like a tax whose revenue is handed to firms). On the international problem, he discusses an internationally harmonised carbon tax, where every country levies the same tax but keeps its own revenue, as a way to bring countries like China and India on board. See the readings section.

What a 2% discount rate means at the petrol pump (a numerical to reproduce)

At the consensus 2% rate, the "right" carbon tax works out to about $190 per tonne of CO2. Is that a lot? The professor translated it into petrol, without a calculator:

  1. Mass: 1 litre of petrol weighs about 750 g (lighter than water's 1,000 g).
  2. Carbon content: petrol is about 85% carbon, so 0.85 × 750 ≈ 640 g of carbon per litre (roughly 15 km in an SUV).
  3. Chemistry: C (12) + O2 (2 × 16 = 32) → CO2 (44). Each gram of carbon becomes 44/12 ≈ 3.67 g of CO2. "People mess this up a lot": the tax is per tonne of CO2, not of carbon.
  4. CO2 per litre: 640 × 44/12 ≈ 2.35 kg (the professor rounded to "about 2 kg").
  5. Tax per litre: $190 per 1,000 kg × ~2.35 kg ≈ $0.45 ≈ ₹40 to 42 per litre.
  6. Meaning: petrol would rise from about ₹100 to ₹142. That is the scale of what India is being asked to accept, and why its negotiators resist.

Three paradoxes of green policy

1. Circularity (chicken and egg)

We use projected growth g to set the discount rate and decide how much to invest in climate action. But if we under-invest, climate damage wrecks the economy and that growth never arrives. g is endogenous; the system is non-linear.

2. Private-social wedge

Market rates reflect private returns, pushed up by taxes and private risk premiums. Using them as the social rate makes us reject long-term projects that are good for society.

3. Myopia

We live in a short-term market economy (quarterly results, election cycles) yet ask firms and governments to make multi-generational choices. This sets up Session 3's short-termism problem.

Externalities: the side-effects nobody pays for

Session 2 has two halves. Part A: how markets brought us here (externalities, open access, the tragedy of the commons). Part B: can markets get us out (property rights, prices, quantities).

The opening puzzle: is this a success story?

The class opened with the FAO chart of world seafood: wild catch flat since the late 1980s while aquaculture (fish farming) soared past it. Keep the question in mind; Module B answers it.

In class: first question on externalities

"What's the negative externality you face most living in this area?" Air pollution: you don't get the clean air you want, and you never chose that. Note the person harmed can even be a market participant: "the point is that you did not choose it."

Definition

An externality is a cost or benefit that affects a third party who did not choose to incur it. Think of it as a side-effect of someone else's economic activity.

Negative externalities (class examples)

  • A coal plant's emissions impose costs on everyone downwind, and on the climate.
  • A farmer's tube well lowers the water table for every neighbour on the same aquifer.
  • Someone playing a video out loud on their phone imposes a cost on everyone within earshot.

Positive externalities

  • Vaccines protect you and also protect people who cannot be vaccinated (herd immunity).
  • Class examples: R&D spillovers ("I benefit but won't capitalise on it"), a new industry creating jobs in a region, following traffic rules, public transport, carpooling, organic farming (less fertiliser runoff for neighbours), recycling, solar panels, technology like UPI.

More negative examples from class

Delhi traffic (also a commons), stubble burning, water depletion, mining, antibiotic overuse.

"Is it an externality?" (class tests)

  • Taxes: inefficient, but "I'm not sure it will fit the definition of an externality."
  • Corruption: creates a bad equilibrium that affects everyone.
  • RBI rate cuts, competition lowering prices: be careful. Ask whether the benefit really lands on someone who did not choose it.
In class: scale decides whether policy should step in

A smoking roommate is a household problem; no public policy needed. Smoking across society raises healthcare costs and hospitalisation, so policy may be justified. Local problems can be solved by local government or by residents organising themselves (a housing society).

The three pictures (draw these in the exam)

Three facts about externalities (slide summary)

  1. Externalities are inevitable in most economic activity.
  2. They destroy the efficiency of the market system in the absence of intervention.
  3. Negative → over-supply by markets; positive → under-supply.

  • Negative: MSC > MPC, Q market > Q efficient. Positive: MSB > MPB, Q market < Q optimal.

Open access and the tragedy of the commons

A commons is a resource open to everyone, where one person's use leaves less for others. It is a special case of externality: market actors harm each other (and maybe bystanders too).

In class: the commons is a prisoner's dilemma

"If everyone follows the rules of the road, we will all get home on time." But each driver gains by cutting in, everyone reasons the same way, and everyone ends up in the jam. Same logic for fish.

Key points from the slides

  • Where a resource is open to all, each user imposes a cost on every other user that no one pays for: a thinner stock, a longer search, a smaller catch.
  • Entry continues until the expected surplus, net of the cost of harvesting, is competed away to zero (the rent is dissipated).
  • Everyone does what is privately optimal, and the result is socially sub-optimal.

Three commons

Antibiotic effectiveness

Every course taken depletes the drug's usefulness for everyone (resistance). Does not come back. In class: Indian doctors prescribe antibiotics very easily, breeding superbugs.

Road space

Each driver delays everyone behind. Resets overnight (a renewable, flow commons).

Fish stocks

Each boat thins the catch for every other boat. Does not come back once collapsed.

The race in numbers (world fisheries)

  • Global wild catch has been flat near 90 million tonnes since the late 1980s, while fishing effort (vessels, engine power, days at sea) kept rising.
  • Catch per unit of effort fell; catch shifted toward smaller, cheaper, shorter-lived species; roughly a third of assessed stocks are fished beyond sustainable limits.
  • Record landings and falling catch per vessel are consistent: total effort is rising faster than the stock can support.
  • Catch is observable, stock health is not. (Hold on to this: Session 3 is all about what can and cannot be observed.)
In class: how students read the chart, and the professor's verdict
  • Flat catch has two explanations. Depletion (effort per boat keeps rising: better ships, even satellites guiding boats to shoals) and regulation (monthly catch limits per boat, bans in the rainy/breeding season). The professor accepted both.
  • You need a second graph: how much fish is in the ocean and what share is caught. "Stock is very difficult to capture... hard enough to know how many humans."
  • Hilsa (a student's example): overfished in the Gangetic delta of West Bengal; its price rises every year, so supply isn't keeping up with demand.
  • Farmed fish may be lower quality (chemicals, fed on wild fish) unless prices reflect quality.
  • Verdict: "a very open market where anyone could have entered, with a lot of demand": the tragedy of the commons, with regulation also playing a role.
Flagged in class: "keep this in mind for every instrument"

Regulators cap the catch (a flow) because they cannot see the stock, like watching the yearly return instead of the whole corpus. Sampling works in a pond; in the open sea it fails. "Any rule or regulation you want to impose, you also need to monitor first." That idea runs straight into Session 3.

Aquaculture: exiting the commons?

Aquaculture grew from about 2 million tonnes in 1960 to 140 million+ today, now larger than wild catch. Flat wild supply plus rising demand raised prices, which induced the substitution of a private good (farmed fish on owned property) for a common-pool good. So the answer to the opening puzzle: partly a success, because ownership fixed incentives. But: growing demand made aquaculture attractive here, which may not hold for every commons, and the high seas continue to be over-exploited.

Flagged in class: substitutes are not always available

"This kind of substitution possibility is not always available. Think about groundwater. If your groundwater runs out", the only alternative is massive, failure-prone infrastructure and messy bargaining over water from elsewhere. Other big market-made problems he listed: climate change, groundwater depletion, road safety, congestion.

Reading 2: "The tragedy of the high seas" (The Economist, 2014)
  • The high seas (beyond national jurisdiction) are a classic unmanaged commons and are deteriorating on several fronts at once.
  • About two-thirds of high-seas fish stocks are over-exploited.
  • Oceans produce about half of Earth's oxygen (mostly via algae); chlorophyll concentrations appear to be falling; dead zones are spreading.
  • Rich countries give about $35 billion a year in fishing subsidies, which make over-fishing worse (a subsidy to depleting a commons).
  • About one-eighth of land is legally protected, but under 1% of the high seas.
  • Proposed fixes: end fishing subsidies, a global vessel registry with unique ID numbers, and more marine reserves.
Reading 3: "Economics focus: A rising tide" (The Economist, 2008)

Reports the Costello, Gaines & Lynham study (Science, 2008) of about 11,000 fisheries: those run with catch shares / individual transferable quotas (ITQs) were about half as likely to collapse, and the benefit grew the longer the system ran. Why: a guaranteed share of a scientifically set total catch ends the "race to fish". Fishers no longer need to grab fish before rivals do, so they fish more safely and profitably, and because their quota is worth more when the stock is healthy, they become stewards of it. This is exactly the property-rights route in the next module.

Remember
  • Open access: entry until surplus is zero; privately optimal, socially sub-optimal.
  • Three commons: antibiotics, road space, fish. Commons = prisoner's dilemma. Regulators watch the flow (catch), not the stock.
  • Wild catch flat ~90 Mt; aquaculture 2 → 140 Mt+ (private good replaces commons). High seas still over-fished.

Route 1: Property rights (Coase)

Part B asks: can markets get us out? Three routes. Property rights is the "market solution"; Pigouvian taxes and quantities are forms of government intervention.

First: non-market instruments (nudges and subsidies)

Before the three market routes, the professor asked for instruments that are not market-based:

Subsidies

Government support for invention, R&D or green technology (fits positive externalities). It "has a flavour of a tax, because the government has to impose something".

Nudges

Information and labels, e.g. telling diners how many people their wasted food could feed; smaller plates. ISB's Operations and Sustainability Council re-enforced a food-waste nudge and a drop of about 15% was reported in class. Road signs against drink-driving are nudges too, but "it's just hard to measure".

The professor's view on nudges

Nudges may work in some settings, especially where people are already bought in (like ISB), but habits erode the effect and, imposed on outsiders, they may be "cheap talk". "Incentives are just a very strong reality of the world. We all look at prices... But if you think [a nudge] can solve the entire problem, maybe not." That is why the course focuses on market instruments.

The Coase theorem (1960)

If property rights are well defined and bargaining is costless, the parties reach the efficient outcome regardless of who holds the right. The assignment decides who pays whom; the quantity of pollution is the same either way.

Why ownership works: a single clear owner of a fishery "has full internalisation of the value of the stock", so thinks about the long run. The owner need not be the fisher.

Real example: Vittel. The French bottled-water company paid €200 per hectare per year to about 40 farmers in its catchment and helped them invest in technologies that kept nitrate levels in the spring water in check. Fertiliser nitrogen becomes nitrates that reach the spring, so the company had to clean the water, pay farmers, or leave. (In class the professor said "per acre" and did not name the company; the slide says Vittel, per hectare. Use the slide.)

In class: three questions about the Vittel case
  1. Who holds the right to the water? Students said the farmers, since they own the land. The professor: water under the land is shared and hard to assign, so it is really an open-access resource all parties can claim.
  2. Which number makes bargaining possible? A student said "200". The professor redirected: the key number is 40. Forty farmers is a few days of meetings; a thousand smallholders in rural India is a different world. "Costless doesn't have to be absolutely zero. It has to be cheap enough."
  3. What must the contract contain? Clear boundaries, numerical limits, unambiguous measurement, and above all monitoring of behaviour or outcomes (test the runoff every year). Without it "you'll realise the water stays polluted, you won't trust the contract, you'll be back to square one". Monitoring costs are transaction costs.

Where it fails: high transaction costs: many parties, hard-to-monitor behaviour, unclear or contested rights.

Case: stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana

Much of Delhi's November pollution comes from crop-residue burning. Combine harvesters leave rice straw across fields. Farmers do not value the straw (as feed or otherwise), and stubble would clog the wheat seeder. Removing it needs labour or machinery, both costly, and the window between rice harvest and wheat sowing is short, so burning is the cheapest option. The class exercise was about property rights only (no fines or taxes): which assignment would you pick, and why?

Option 1: farmers have the right to burn; Delhi pays them not to

  • Hard to enforce: how do you verify compliance (that someone did not burn)?
  • Who pays?
  • How can millions of farmers bargain with millions of citizens?

Option 2: Delhi has the right to clean air; farmers pay to burn

  • Easier: non-compliance is easier to observe than compliance (satellites spot fires, though imperfectly).
  • But who does a burning farmer pay?
  • Bargaining is still just as hard.
In class: the debate
  • "Delhi's people should not be paying": that is Option 2, which the class leaned towards (without full agreement).
  • "Tomorrow I say I'll burn my garbage, pay me": the professor called it "a great point". "Why should there be a right in law is an important question."
  • Clean-air legislation sets air-quality standards; no law grants a right to pollute ("absolutely right").
  • One student argued neither option works: alternatives are not viable for farmers and the crop gap is too short, so government must step in.

What actually happens

  • Farmers pay to burn, but as a penalty to the government, with daily satellite monitoring during the season.
  • Fines: ₹5,000 per incident for holdings under 2 acres, ₹10,000 for 2 to 5 acres, ₹30,000 above 5 acres.
  • Implementation is harder than formulation: in 2023 Punjab's pollution board imposed about ₹2.4 crore on 9,258 farmers and recovered only 11%.
  • But it is somewhat working: 36,663 fires in 2023 → 10,909 in 2024 (slide figures). In class the professor was more cautious: burning has fallen in Punjab over 5 to 10 years, but "whether that's a result of these technological changes or [policy], it's not quite clear".
  • Monitoring can be gamed: farmers seem to know when the satellite passes overhead.
  • Other measures mentioned in class: NTPC must source pellets from stubble-burning areas; shorter-duration rice varieties leave more time before wheat sowing.
  • Notice: a flavour of Coase (implicit recognition of the right to clean air), but the government acts on behalf of citizens and there is no negotiated price.

Property rights to nations: enclosing the sea

  • UNCLOS (1982) created 200-mile Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZs), enclosing roughly a third of the ocean. Each country got jurisdiction; management still required domestic action. The class example: an Indian boat can no longer fish up to Sri Lanka's coast, but "Sri Lankan fishermen are still allowed to catch as much as they want" inside Sri Lankan waters. Open access shrinks, it does not vanish.
  • Individual transferable quotas (ITQs): total catch is set from scientific estimates; fishers own and trade permits.
  • Alaska halibut ("a very endangered species"): a total catch cap, permits allocated to fishers, trading allowed: "property rights plus a cap and trade together". The season went from a 2 to 3 day free-for-all to eight months; nobody is in a rush and fishing is safer. Catch-share fisheries are roughly half as likely to collapse (slide). In class he also said the catch "falls to half the previous" amount under the cap; for the exam, quote the slide's "half as likely to collapse".
  • Notice: the right went to a country, not to the directly affected parties. Each EEZ turned a global commons into something like domestic territory, and countries still had to act against over-fishing inside it.
  • Recent innovations: Nepal conferred forest rights on local communities; Bangladesh made rivers legal persons with a guardian to act for them.
Remember
  • Coase: clear rights + costless bargaining ⇒ efficient outcome whoever holds the right; assignment only changes who pays.
  • Fails with many parties, poor monitoring, contested rights (stubble burning).
  • Vittel (€200/ha/yr, ~40 farmers: the key number is 40, and monitoring is essential); EEZs (UNCLOS 1982); ITQs (Alaska halibut 2-3 days → 8 months). Nudges and subsidies help but don't replace incentives.

Route 2: Prices, a.k.a. the Pigouvian tax

Internalise the externality: change incentives so that people account for the harm in their own decisions.

  • The price of the good understates its true cost, because damage to third parties costs the buyer and seller nothing.
  • Pigouvian tax (after the Cambridge economist A.C. Pigou, about 100 years ago): a per-unit tax equal to the marginal external damage. The polluter now pays for harm the way it pays for labour or fuel.
  • Setting it needs the curves, which governments rarely know, so in practice they guess from rules of thumb and cost data from some firms.
  • With the tax in place, private incentives line up with social ones.
  • It is a price intervention: government changes the price, and that indirectly brings quantity to the efficient level.

Why a tax is clever: firms sort themselves

  • Abatement cost is just another input cost: the cost of cutting pollution. It is private information. More productive firms (better processes, better managers) are likely to have lower MACs.
  • The government is unlikely to know each producer's marginal abatement cost (MAC), and MACs differ across firms.
  • Under a tax, each firm chooses efficiently: abate if MAC < tax, pay the tax if MAC > tax. In the stubble example, farmers with high MAC (no cheap alternative) pay the fine.
  • The same tax hits firms differently: an efficient firm absorbs it and cuts pollution; an inefficient firm may barely abate or even exit the market.
  • Firms may pass the tax on to customers, so it changes the price for everyone.
  • The catch: because the government does not know MACs, it cannot predict how much abatement the tax will produce. Price is certain; quantity is not.
  • Revenue goes to the government, which can recycle it, but "there's no way to control what they do" with it.
It is not a sin tax

"The idea behind a Pigouvian tax is not to tax sinful activities." It is perfectly legal to burn stubble on your farm. The point is to correct the price of something that is mispriced, not to punish people. If an exam asks "is a carbon tax a punishment?", this is your line.

Contrast (from class): cigarette taxes exist for two reasons: an externality (others are harmed) and the harm smokers do to their own future selves, which also burdens the healthcare system. Economists call that second part an internality (the professor described it without using the word). A pure Pigouvian tax targets only the harm to others.

Reading 4: N. Gregory Mankiw, "One answer to global warming: a new tax" (NYT, Sept 2007)
  • Economists across the spectrum (the "Pigou Club") agree the most efficient response to climate change is a tax on carbon: it puts a price on the externality and lets millions of households and firms find the cheapest ways to cut emissions.
  • It raises revenue, which can be returned by cutting other taxes (e.g. income or payroll) or via rebates, so it need not grow government.
  • A tax gives price certainty and is transparent. Cap and trade is its "quantity" cousin; if permits are handed out free, it is like a carbon tax whose revenue is given to incumbent firms. Politicians prefer it partly because the cost is less visible ("tax" is a dirty word).
  • International angle: fast-growing countries like China and India will resist caps that constrain growth. An internationally harmonised carbon tax, where every country applies the same tax and keeps its own revenue, is a possible bridge. (Links to Session 1's "who should pay".)
Remember
  • Pigouvian tax = marginal external damage per unit; aligns private and social incentives.
  • Firms with MAC < tax abate; others pay. Efficient, but abatement quantity is uncertain.
  • Corrects a price; does not punish a sin.

Route 3: Quantities, mandates and cap and trade

Sometimes the government wants to choose the quantity of pollution directly. A flat quota is inefficient; cap and trade fixes that.

Mandates and standards

In class: when a mandate beats any market tool

How is nuclear waste regulated? It has zero benefit and maximum damage, so any amount is harmful. Efficiency almost never means zero production, and even with a tax a firm would still choose some waste. So for catastrophic risks we use mandates or standards (e.g. store waste far from people). Session 3 adds a second case: when abatement is almost free (HFC-23), just mandate it. A mandate (how something must be done) is different from a uniform standard ("everyone cut 10 units"), which is inefficient because 10 units is easy for some firms and very hard for others.

A fixed quota or uniform standard on every producer ignores that firms have very different marginal abatement costs. Forcing the same cut on a firm that can abate cheaply and one that cannot wastes money. It is particularly problematic for growing economies (a fixed quota blocks new, efficient entrants). Answer: cap and trade.

How cap and trade works

  1. Set a cap on total pollution across the industry.
  2. Producers need a permit for every unit of pollution. Cap = total permits.
  3. Permits are tradable; the market sets the permit price.
  4. Firms with cheap abatement cut more and sell spare permits; firms with expensive abatement buy. Trading continues until MACs are equal, which is the least-cost way to hit the cap.

Examples: EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS); India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS); the US sulphur dioxide market (1995), which cut emissions at a fraction of the projected cost.

Cap and trade in the wild

US SO2 (acid rain) programme, 1995

High-sulphur coal → SO2 → acid rain (dilute sulphuric acid) on the US east coast. Instead of mandating equipment or equal cuts, the US capped total SO2, allocated permits across coal plants and let them trade: pollution fell at least cost, firms stayed in business and had time to invest. Cut emissions at a fraction of the projected cost.

EU ETS

In class: running for about 20 years and "the one mechanism where you get a reliable, credible price of carbon"; the price "used to be zero at the beginning", now around €80. Phase 1 over-allocation crashed the price to near zero (2007). After reform (verified baselines, auctioning, the Market Stability Reserve) the price went above €80 in 2022-23, with power-sector emissions falling. (Session 3 dissects the crash.)

In class: where else could cap and trade work?
  • India has started a CO2 cap and trade (the CCTS on the slide).
  • Endorsed ideas: deforestation; water use by future data centres.
  • The EU's CBAM (carbon border tax on steel and cement): "more like a trade mechanism... like a tariff almost".
  • A student's 1% blending rule (blend biogas into natural gas; shortfall firms buy credits from over-achievers): the professor said it works like cap and trade, but 1% is a floor, not a cap, and "it's always the trade mechanism that is allowing you to reduce cost".
Reading 6: "ETS, RIP?" (The Economist, April 2013)

Written when the EU carbon price had slumped to around €3 to €5 a tonne: far too low to change any investment decision. Causes: generous free allocation after lobbying by member states and heavy industry, the post-2008 recession cutting output (so emissions fell without effort), and a flood of cheap international offset credits (including CDM credits like the HFC-23 ones in Session 3). The European Parliament initially rejected "backloading" (temporarily withholding about 900 million allowances) in April 2013, which is why the piece asked whether the scheme was dead. Backloading later passed and the Market Stability Reserve followed, which is the reform the slides credit for the later price recovery. Lesson: a cap only works if it binds.

Remember
  • Cap = number of permits; permits trade; market sets price.
  • Trade until MAC1 = MAC2: the cap is met at least total cost.
  • US SO2 success; EU ETS Phase 1 crash then reform (>€80 in 2022-23).

The slope of the damage curve decides

Coal vs groundwater: substitutes decide (class)

Coal has substitutes: if it gets too expensive, people switch to renewables, so a price works. Groundwater has no substitute, so a tax is too uncertain: you want a limit on quantity. Truly catastrophic cases (nuclear waste) get mandates.

India's hidden groundwater cap (class)

Farm electricity is free or flat-rate, so states ration water by rationing power (e.g. four hours a day; a tighter cap = fewer hours). Farmers cheat: store water, fit higher-horsepower motors, run diesel generators. So the regulator must also watch pump sizes, diesel connections and diesel sales.

The one-line rule (slide 40)

CO2 → relatively flat marginal damage → tax is attractive. Collapse threshold → steep marginal damage → cap is attractive. This is Martin Weitzman's "prices vs quantities" logic (1974), worth naming for extra credit.

Closing: economics vs political economy

  • Economics: tax vs cap depends on which mistake is costly.
  • Political economy: who bears the uncertainty? (my reading: under a tax the environment bears quantity risk; under a cap firms bear price risk) and who gets the rents?
  • In class, on rents: free permits are a free asset. Firms that got many EU ETS permits 20 years ago grew rich as the price rose from zero to ~€80. Firms with connections and bargaining power win more permits; allocating by past emissions puts you "back in the land of negotiating". Under a cap the government only monitors and enforces and gets no revenue; under a tax it gets all the revenue, which is itself a reason some governments prefer a tax.
  • Cheating is universal. Every instrument needs monitoring and enforcement, inside firms too ("an incentive to cheat upwards towards the manager"). But the aim is not zero cheating: even in Europe about 5% of electricity bills go unpaid, and chasing that to zero would cost a fortune. "Reduce cheating to a point where it's sort of irrelevant." (A narrow income-tax base, as in India, is design, not evasion.)
  • Teaser for Session 3: "what happens after the instrument is chosen".
Remember
  • Tax fixes price (quantity uncertain); cap fixes quantity (cost uncertain).
  • Flat damage → tax; steep/threshold damage → cap.
  • Politics: who bears uncertainty, who captures rents (connected firms win free permits; tax revenue tempts governments).
  • No substitute (groundwater) or threshold (fish) → cap; catastrophic (nuclear waste) → mandate.

Session 3 · Module B · Readings 5 & 6

Failure 1: Moral hazard and the endogenous baseline

The most famous case in the course. A subsidy for destroying a waste gas made producing the waste gas profitable.

HFC-23: the background

  • HCFC-22 is a common refrigerant (for ACs and fridges). Making it produces a waste gas, HFC-23 (about 1.5 to 3% by mass per the slide diagram; "1 to 3%" in class).
  • HFC-23 is an extremely potent greenhouse gas: about 11,700 to 12,000 times CO2 (its global warming potential; methane is 21, nitrous oxide 310 on the same UN scale).
  • It is cheap to destroy: incinerate it for under €1 per tonne of CO2-equivalent.
  • Under the UN's Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) (created under the Kyoto Protocol), projects in developing countries that cut emissions earn tradable carbon credits. Destroying one tonne of HFC-23 earned 11,700 credits.
  • At €10 to €20 per credit, every tonne destroyed earned credits worth over €100,000. Destruction cost vs reward: roughly 1 to 100 or worse.
  • Why pay at all? Destroying it costs "a few cents", but firms still would not do it unprompted. The credit acted as a subsidy, and a far too strong one: the principal either does not get less HFC-23, or gets it "but you're just paying too much for it".

What happened

  • The subsidy paid for destroying waste gas, but plants decided how much waste gas to produce. More refrigerant → more waste gas → more credits.
  • At peak prices, credit revenue exceeded refrigerant revenue (Stanford's Michael Wara: nearly twice as much).
  • Nineteen plants (11 in China, 5 in India) earned about half of all early CDM credits (the NYT reports 46% of all credits since 2005, from plants in Argentina, China, India, Mexico and South Korea).
  • Plant data showed waste-gas ratios sitting at the maximum allowed during crediting periods, and falling when credits stopped. Some plants ran only until they hit the maximum creditable output, then shut until next year; some used inefficient processes to make more waste.

Naming the failure

Endogenous baseline

The baseline (how much would have been emitted anyway) was something the agent could move. The counterfactual here was not hidden: pre-CDM production data and known abatement costs showed what plants could have done. The problem was behaviour change caused by the payment.

Moral hazard

The agent's action responds to the contract in a way that is sub-optimal for the principal. More broadly: one party takes more risk (or behaves worse) because someone else bears the cost.

Examples from class: an SUV as "implicit insurance" (you're less likely to be hurt in a crash, so you drive more riskily); free nationalised healthcare (a check-up every morning for a flu); corporate expense accounts; company-paid flights booked last minute; electronics insurance; an MBA someone else pays for; better car insurance, so you stop minding small dents; banks that are "too big to fail", "a classic example from the banking situation". Rule of thumb: "anything that you can insure changes how you behave."

In class: why the endogenous baseline makes moral hazard worse

"These problems go hand in hand. Endogenous baselines make moral hazard worse." If the regulator doesn't know what a firm produced before, the firm has an incentive to lie. The professor's key question: why didn't the CDM use the verified pre-CDM production data as the baseline? "You have all of that data. If you use that as a baseline that's actually verified, you can maybe account for some differences, but not massive differences." And a warning for the whole course: "any contract you write, you are potentially inducing moral hazard for the agent."

Reading 5: "Profits on carbon credits drive output of a harmful gas" (NYT, Aug 2012)
  • Each plant earned on average $20 to $40 million a year from destroying waste gas, "largely pure profit". Credits were worth "nearly 100 times more" than the cost of destruction (David Doniger, NRDC: "no one had this in mind").
  • Over-production of HCFC-22 kept coolant prices low, which discouraged a switch to less damaging coolants, and HCFC-22 itself warms the climate and depletes ozone.
  • In India, coolant plants got about half of all CDM credits in the first five years (e.g. Gujarat Fluorochemicals, which listed carbon credits as a business line).
  • Fixes attempted: UN refused credits to new plants from 2007; cut creditable waste from 3% to 1% of coolant output; the EU banned HFC-23 credits from its ETS from 2013 ("counterfeit currency").
  • Still, in 2012, 18% of credits went to the 19 coolant plants vs 12% to 2,372 wind farms and 0.2% to 312 solar projects.
  • The trap once a subsidy is entrenched: plants threatened to vent the gas if payments stopped (legal in China and India then). Money creates lobbying power (capture).

Compounding failure: response to ratcheting

If tomorrow's target depends on today's performance, today's performance is not honest. An agent who over-performs reveals capability and gets a harder target next round. So it holds back, or inflates the baseline before the target is set.

In class: ratcheting from students' own jobs

Students described managers raising targets after a good year; one negotiated the rise "halfway"; another said the baseline keeps climbing until you do far more than expected. The professor: "good performance in a year implies the targets are raised for you. That's what I mean by ratcheting." The agent resists because it means more work without matching pay, and the manager is often an agent of the CEO too. In the chart, the manager wants the index at 80 in year one; the negotiated gradual (blue) path suits the agent but not a firm that wants aggressive cuts. The principal's options: pay people more, or change processes.

Inflating the baseline at country scale: EU ETS Phase 1 (2005-07)

  • Each member state set its own allocation of permits, based on projections supplied largely by the regulated industries. Permits were free.
  • Each state had reason to be generous to its own firms; each firm had reason to overstate its emissions.
  • April 2006: first verified 2005 emissions came in below the allocation. The price fell from about €30 to under €10 within days, and to near zero by late 2007. Phase 1 permits could not be banked into Phase 2, so they became worthless, while Phase 2 futures held up around €15 to €20.
  • From your lecture notes: Phase 1 was intentionally "learning by doing", a trial phase to build the data and infrastructure. The crash exposed the oversupply the self-reported baselines had created.
In class: the full EU ETS story
  • The EU learned from the CDM being gamed, so it built the ETS in phases, with Phase 1 (2005-07) as explicit "learning by doing" to find "chinks in the armour".
  • The cap can come from science, but "who gets the permits is the key decision". In Phase 1, self-reporting happened at two levels: member states told the EU how many permits they needed, and each state let its firms say how many they wanted. "So there is an over-allocation of permits, of course." Firms essentially claimed they couldn't cut.
  • Verifying actual emissions meant surveying every firm; that took until mid-2006. The price had started around €5, climbed to almost €30, then "halves in a month" and kept falling.
  • Why it hit zero: Phase 1 was quarantined by design. Permits could not carry into Phase 2, and Phase 2 (from January 2008) allocated on verified emissions. "In the first phase, whatever really happens, it will not affect anything."
  • Phase 2 futures also wobbled at first over credibility, then rose once supply meant something.
  • Verdict: "a slightly expensive way of learning by doing. But it was a good idea." Twenty years on, "it's the one market where you can actually figure out the price of carbon just by looking at trading."

Design the contract: how do you avoid moral hazard here?

From the slides

  • Pay against a baseline the agent cannot move (e.g. a pre-policy baseline).
  • Mandate when abatement is cheap (HFC-23 destruction costs under €1: just require it, maybe with a small subsidy).
  • Benchmark on what the technology can do, not on what the agent did last year (kills the ratchet).

Answers the professor endorsed in class

  • Third-party governance, with penalties also set by a third party.
  • Documented baseline: firms must "show actual papers from beforehand", not just self-report.
  • Technology benchmarks, e.g. fleet fuel efficiency: you know what's typical, so claims "can't go beyond crazy numbers".
  • Mandate in two cases: catastrophic risk (the Session 2 nuclear-waste logic) and very cheap abatement (HFC-23): "just mandate... we will pay you some amount" rather than allow trading.
  • "These are always works in progress. You have to figure out in that particular situation what you want to do."

(Your earlier notes also listed "asymmetric rewards" and "forced baseline data sharing"; those are fair ideas but do not appear in the transcript.)

Remember
  • HFC-23: 11,700 credits/t, <€1/t to destroy, >€100k of credits/t; 19 plants (11 China, 5 India) got ~half of early CDM credits. Should have used the verified pre-CDM baseline.
  • Endogenous baseline + moral hazard; ratcheting makes agents hide capability.
  • EU ETS Phase 1: self-reported, free, generous → €30 to <€10 in days (Apr 2006) → ~0 by 2007.

Failure 2: Are you measuring what you care about?

Three versions: the agent optimises the short term (2a), effort goes only to what is measured (2b), and the boundary gets moved (2c).

2a. Short-termism: the chiller

A plant manager at a food processing plant must replace a chiller. Standard: ₹40 lakh. High-efficiency: ₹58 lakh. The efficient chiller saves ₹6 lakh a year for 15 years. But the manager's bonus depends on this year's profit. The extra ₹18 lakh hurts this year's bonus; the future savings do not add to it. So the manager buys the standard chiller, even though the firm (the principal) cares about the present value of future earnings.

Design the contract: reward long-term thinking

From the slides

  • Bonuses on 3 to 5 year horizons.
  • Clawback provisions to recoup bonuses or equity if accounting manipulation or excessive risk-taking is discovered.
  • Non-financial metrics: workforce retention, customer satisfaction, R&D milestones, safety outcomes.

What was said in class

  • Book the chiller as capex (a student's idea) so it is spread over years and does not hit this year's profit, while the ₹6 lakh savings still flow. Professor: "a nice creative accounting way of solving that problem... the idea is to let it not sit in the wrong place for the manager."
  • Base the bonus on profits over time, or on estimated cash flows ("a nice operationalisation").
  • RSUs vesting over about five years: "almost like an equity approach".
  • Non-financial metrics: workforce retention (a proxy for how happy people are), customer satisfaction (customer-facing roles), R&D milestones. Caveat: "It's not going to work for everyone."
  • Clawbacks: C-suite bankers took big risks and had often left before the problems surfaced. A clawback triggered by fraud, manipulation or excess risk-taking recovers equity or salary, but must be enforced in court, so it's "potentially hard to implement"; its value is mainly as a deterrent.

2b. Multi-task: effort goes to what is measured

An agent does several tasks; some can be measured, some cannot. Reward the measurable ones and effort moves away from the unmeasurable ones. The stronger the reward, the bigger the distortion. (Holmström and Milgrom's multi-task problem.) Keep two columns in mind: what the target rewards, and what the principal wants. Effort goes to the first.

Example

A plant manager is responsible for (1) emissions abated in production and (2) personal and process safety. The sustainability-linked bonus is based entirely on emissions permits earned. Result: the manager works only on emissions and neglects safety. The principal cares about both.

Class prompt and answers

"Think of your last job. What did your appraisal measure? What did your employer actually want?"

  • Loan quantity vs credit quality: bank staff got increments on loans disbursed, not on credit quality. "It's easier to measure quantity, not so easy to verify quality. So the effort goes into the quantity."
  • Visibility: under forced-ranking quotas, strong performers lose to peers with better visibility, so "visibility is also an outcome" and effort shifts into being seen.
  • Sales first, everything else later; moving goalposts.
The professor's honest answer on multi-tasking

"There's no really good answers for this multitasking problem." First, the principal must decide what it actually cares about: if it truly wants sales and loan targets, there is little to fix. Appraisals get about 15 minutes plus forms, so pare down the number of measures while keeping them broad enough.

Design the contract: avoid multi-tasking and boundary games

  • Align incentives across all KPIs and verticals (if one vertical wants more vehicles and another fewer emissions, pay them together).
  • Write targets on all scopes.
  • Recalculate the whole footprint before and after a divestment, "as if you didn't have the coal plant", so the comparison is fair.
  • Keep the incentive weak: if one task is measurable and another isn't but matters, don't pile reward on the measurable one.

Multi-task at country scale: India's NDC

  • NDCs are informal, non-enforceable Paris commitments, and the 45% target is on intensity (emissions per unit of GDP), not total emissions.
  • Why the gap persists: most power is sold under 25-year bilateral power purchase agreements at fixed prices, hard to break for renewables; only about 8% of India's power trades on wholesale or day-ahead markets; renewables sit far from demand and transmission is lacking. "You're not going to try and solve those problems if you don't have a target for them."

Failure 3: You cannot verify what you are buying (additionality)

Adverse selection before the fact, and unverifiable counterfactuals after it. Carbon offsets are the textbook example.

In class: the airline offset

Buying a ticket from a European airline, you're offered an offset for the flight's CO2. "Most of that is a scam. And the reason for that is additionality." The professor's buyer advice: never buy an offset with an unobservable baseline unless you know it works.

  • Before effort is chosen, the agent already knows something the principal does not: its own abatement cost, its true baseline. Suppliers self-report emissions; internal capex bids self-report abatement costs. The agent misreports in the direction that benefits it most. (This is hidden information / adverse selection, as opposed to hidden action / moral hazard.)
  • After the fact, the principal cannot tell if claimed abatement is additional. Additionality: would the claimed abatement have happened anyway? The counterfactual is unknown.

Offsets and REDD+

  • Demand side: a firm short of its target buys REDD+ credits (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and forest Degradation).
  • These pay landowners for avoided deforestation: the forest that would have been cut without the payment.
  • Supply side: private conservation companies (e.g. Wildlife Works) forecast a baseline of clearing, protect the forest, and earn credits = baseline minus actual.
  • The baseline is a forecast of something that, by construction, never happens. It is hard to check.
  • Class illustration: a company claims 100 units of avoided deforestation; if the real figure is 10, it has put 100 offsets on the market instead of 10. The payment rides on the claimant's own claim, which "creates its own moral hazard issue": Failure 3 feeds back into Failure 1.
  • Third-party certifiers such as Verra certify about three of every four voluntary carbon credits worldwide.

Actual cover falls about 12% in 10 years; a matched control area (similar forest nearby, no project) falls about 16%. Only that gap is real avoided deforestation. Everything above it comes from an inflated baseline.

Choosing the counterfactual: Madre de Dios, Peru

A Verra-certified Amazon project (reported by The Guardian, Jan 2023). The reference area used to project deforestation included a major road and a town, where deforestation naturally runs fast. The project area itself, remote, saw almost no deforestation. Choosing a high-deforestation reference area inflates the baseline and therefore the credits.

In class: the drug-trial logic

You can never observe the project area without the project. So, as in a randomised controlled trial with a placebo group, you need a control group: some other forest. "So this is how you game the system: you find a control group that has very high deforestation." Roads matter because (a) you can get heavy machinery in and (b) you can get the logs out to market. No road, little logging.

The offset market, from inside (class role-play)

You are the supplier

Payment = baseline minus actual. What baseline do you report? The highest one you can defend.

You are the certifier

Paid 10 cents per credit issued. What do you check? Your pay rises with credits issued: a conflict of interest.

You are the buyer

You have a net-zero pledge to meet. Would you pay? What would make you confident? The temptation is cheap credits, but the professor's answer: refuse unverifiable offsets. If demand changes, supply has to.

The supplier and certifier both gain from a high baseline, and the system let the supplier set its own. Verra, the leading certifier, was paid 10 cents per credit issued, so "they are definitely not going to question the claims as effectively as they should". This led to a "massive scandal": the third-party fix failed because the certifier's own contract was written wrongly. (The slide's role-play was not run in class; these answers came from the discussion.)

Design the contract: avoid the additionality failure

  • Do not pay for an unobservable baseline.
  • Pay the certifier a fixed fee, not per credit (high enough to fund real work; could be graded by project size).
  • Verra's 2023 fix: baselines set centrally by a Verra technical team, not by the developer. The professor: "I don't know if they have changed the incentive problem." It also needs strong technical capacity.
  • A government body could set baselines, but it "could also get captured if the conservation company is big"; workable where institutions are strong.
  • Demand side: buyers refuse unverifiable offsets.
  • Insist on verifiability (matched control areas, satellite data, independent checks).

Additionality is not only a CO2 issue: it can arise when designing markets for local pollutants too.

Remember
  • Additionality = would it have happened anyway? The counterfactual is unknown.
  • REDD+ credits = baseline − actual; baseline is a never-observed forecast.
  • Supplier and certifier (paid 10 cents per credit) gain from high baselines → Verra scandal. Fix: fixed certifier fee, central baselines (Verra 2023), verifiability, buyers who refuse.

Failure 4: Will the rules hold? Enforcement and time inconsistency

A contract nobody enforces is a suggestion. A rule everyone expects to be abandoned changes nothing.

4a. Enforcement: Becker's condition

Cheat if   gain from cheating > p × Fp = probability of being caught (raised by monitoring) · F = penalty. Both cost the principal. (Gary Becker's economics of crime.)
  • In the firm: a supplier code of conduct with announced audits has low p. A sustainability KPI that no one ever loses a bonus over has F = 0.
  • You can economise on monitoring by raising the penalty, up to the point where the penalty is not credible (nobody believes you will really shut a supplier or jail a farmer).
  • "Cheating" here is used loosely: often it is simply a rational response to a low penalty on a legal obligation, not dishonesty.
In class: an infinite penalty can mean zero enforcement

Under Indian pollution law, a large plant that keeps violating norms can be shut down: F is effectively infinite. "How likely do you think it is that a firm is shut down?", especially one producing useful things. Nobody believes it, so the threat is not credible. "F can also be way too high... because that changes the probability itself of enforcement."

In class: reading Becker's line
  • RECs: the purchase obligation is in law, and everyone can see how many certificates are bought, so p = 1. But the penalty is zero, so perfect monitoring still gets no compliance.
  • PAT: the penalty is below the cost of complying (changing processes, engaging workers): "it's not worth our time". (The professor described PAT as the successor of the appliance star-rating scheme; the standard description is BEE's scheme for energy-intensive industry. Know both.)
  • EU ETS after reform: p high (verified data on past behaviour, ground surveys) and F "real but not insane": a graded penalty on excess emissions. A shutdown threat would not be credible even with high p.
  • Montreal Protocol (1987), to protect stratospheric ozone from CFCs: "entirely voluntary", yet it worked because (a) substitute technology arrived and (b) the sanction was trade-based. Cross-border trade in refrigerants is observable (p ≈ 1) and tariffs were high enough to change behaviour. Once big countries enforced it, everyone had to follow; the ozone layer has been healing over decades.
  • p and F are complements: design them together. Perfect p with zero F (RECs) fails; huge F that nobody imposes (shutdown) fails.

Decoding the Indian examples: RECs are Renewable Energy Certificates; distribution companies and large consumers have a Renewable Purchase Obligation (RPO) to buy a share of renewable power or certificates, but shortfalls have rarely been penalised. PAT is the Bureau of Energy Efficiency's scheme for energy-intensive industries: plants get energy-efficiency targets, over-achievers earn tradable "ESCerts", laggards must buy them or pay a penalty; if the penalty is below the certificate cost, paying is cheaper than complying.

4b. Time inconsistency

  • A principal announces a rule today that it will want to abandon tomorrow, and agents know it.
  • In the firm: a sustainability programme funded from the discretionary budget gets cut in the first bad quarter. Everyone knows it, so no one invests around it.
  • The reverse also holds: a credible commitment changes behaviour before any rule binds. Class example: if the UNFCCC credibly announced that Scope 3 targets will be enforced in 5 years, firms would start looking for lower-emission suppliers today, "and there was nothing forcing you".
  • Empty promises from class: Yamuna clean-up pledges ("everyone who lives around the Yamuna knows nothing is going to happen"); school election candidates all promising air-conditioned classrooms until the principal said there was no budget. Such promises "you can completely ignore".

Design the contract: avoid enforcement and time-inconsistency failures

  • Systems that work spend on p, because raising penalties very high is hard: continuous emissions monitors (made tamper-proof), third-party verification (with its own incentives and expertise checked, the Verra lesson). MRV = Monitoring, Reporting, Verification.
  • Institutions make commitments credible: a rule that binds the principal too, an independent monitor, a public number that is hard to walk back.

UK Infrastructure Commission

The professor's example: a non-partisan body with a legal basis that gives technical guidance and sets budgets for long-term infrastructure, taking election-cycle politics out. Weakness: some political appointees. The model suits long-horizon areas: education, healthcare, sustainability.

Checks and balances

An institution that binds the principal: in the US, Congress holds the purse (the President must sell spending to it) and courts can strike down unconstitutional laws (harder under the current presidency, he noted).

Inside the firm

Independent directors: "their job is to question bad decisions". Building such checks lower down the organisation is harder.

Reading 7 · Getting Strategic About Sustainability (four lenses)

The problem: companies try to tackle too many sustainability issues at once and spread themselves thin; overpromising can even look like greenwashing. The reason: the standard tool, the materiality matrix (firm's interests on one axis, stakeholders' and society's on the other), produces a bloated list. It becomes an "everything is material" matrix (Alison Taylor, NYU). The authors studied 256 sustainability reports (2022): firms named on average 11.8 top-priority issues (range 2 to 43). Strategy experts, drawing on neuroscience (the brain handles 3 to 5 items at once), recommend no more than six. "Tensioned topics" are issues material from one perspective but not the other (usually socially material with no business case).

The solution: hold two tensions (outside-in vs inside-out; perception vs reality, i.e. subjective vs objective) and screen every issue through four lenses. Issues that appear in all four are the company's critical sustainability issues.

Why do Stern and Nordhaus reach opposite climate policy conclusions despite using similar science? S1 · 10 marks

Define. Both evaluate climate policy with cost-benefit analysis in which future damages are converted to present value using a social discount rate r. By the Ramsey rule, r = δ + ηg.

Mechanism. Stern (ethicist) sets δ = 0.1% (discounting people for living later is unethical), η = 1, g = 1.3%, so r = 1.4%. Nordhaus (positivist) calibrates to market behaviour: δ = 1.5%, η = 2, g = 2%, so r = 5.5%. Compounded over centuries, the four-point gap is enormous: Stern values a harm 100 years out about 53 times more, and at 200 years about 2,800 times more. Nordhaus rerunning his model with Stern's rate reproduced Stern's advice, confirming the rate drives the result.

Case. The class example: $100 trillion of damage in 100 years. At 1.4% money doubles every 50 years, so PV = 100/4 = $25 trillion worth spending today. At 5.5% it doubles every ~13 years, about 8 doublings, so PV = 100/256 ≈ $0.4 trillion. A rate roughly four times higher shrinks the answer about 64-fold (53-fold exactly). At 7%, a $1 trillion loss in 100 years justifies only ~$1.15 bn. Note too that Nordhaus uses the higher η (2), so the "positivist" is more egalitarian than the "ethicist": labels mislead, parameters clarify.

Limitation. δ and η are value judgments, not facts; the Ramsey form rests on convenient assumptions. Uncertainty argues for rates near the low end (averaging PVs, e.g. 10%/2% → 3.4%), and growth itself may be damaged by climate (circularity).

Recommend / link. Weisbach & Sunstein: the fight mixes two questions. Ethics should set how much we leave the future; the market rate (adjusted for uncertainty) should choose projects. So argue for a larger legacy, not a lower rate for every green project.

"Refusing to discount helps future generations." Critically evaluate. S1 · 10 marks

For: intergenerational neutrality (Ramsey: discounting is "ethically indefensible"; Rawls' veil of ignorance); market rates reflect private, short horizons and ignore posterity; private returns may understate social returns.

Against (W&S): discounting prices opportunity cost. $100 bn in a 1.4% project yields $400 bn in 100 years; at 5.5% it would yield $21 tn, or $2 bn would deliver the same $400 bn. Choosing the low-return project wastes resources and can harm the future. Not discounting at all (Koopmans) implies saving everything, an absurd savings rate no democracy would adopt. We discount money, not lives: a $5 m statistical life in 200 years needs only its present value set aside today.

Nuance: Ricardian equivalence means private savers may offset government projects; Lind argues we cannot store money for 200 years, but the same problem hits abatement. Under uncertainty, use a low rate.

Conclusion: refusing to discount is "a singularly crude way" to help the future. Better: raise the overall legacy, then pick the highest-return projects, climate abatement included only if it clears the bar. Sentiment sets the size of the gift; arithmetic decides what's in the box.

Numerical: the growth rate is 2% or 3% with equal probability. What discount rate should you use for a benefit 100 years away? S1 · 5 marks

Average present values, not rates. PV per ₹1: 0.5/1.02100 + 0.5/1.03100 = 0.5(0.1380) + 0.5(0.0520) = 0.0950. Certainty-equivalent rate: (1/0.0950)1/100 − 1 ≈ 2.38%, below the naive 2.5%. Reason: PV is convex in r, so the low-rate (bad-growth) scenario dominates. Implication: be conservative on growth for long horizons (Weitzman: discount the far future at its lowest plausible rate).

Explain why markets over-exploit fisheries and compare three remedies. S2 · 10 marks

Define/mechanism. Open-access resource: each boat keeps its catch but imposes a thinner stock, longer search and smaller catch on others. Entry continues until surplus net of harvest cost is competed to zero. Privately optimal, socially sub-optimal (tragedy of the commons, a special case of externality where actors harm each other).

Evidence. Wild catch flat near 90 Mt since the late 1980s while effort rose; catch per unit effort fell; ~1/3 of assessed stocks overfished; two-thirds of high-seas stocks over-exploited, worsened by ~$35 bn of rich-country subsidies. Catch is observable; stock health is not.

Remedy 1: property rights. Coase: with clear rights and cheap bargaining the efficient outcome emerges. UNCLOS 1982 EEZs enclosed a third of the ocean; ITQs give fishers tradable shares of a scientific total catch. Alaska halibut: season from 2-3 days to 8 months; catch-share fisheries about half as likely to collapse (A rising tide). Aquaculture (2 → 140 Mt+) replaced a commons with a private good. Limits: the high seas still have no owner.

Remedy 2: tax per unit of catch. Prices the externality, but regulators do not know the stock's threshold, so quantity is uncertain.

Remedy 3: quantity cap (TAC with tradable quotas). Near a collapse threshold, marginal damage is steep, so getting quantity wrong is disastrous. A cap is preferred (tax vs cap logic).

Recommend. Cap + tradable quotas inside EEZs, end subsidies, global vessel registry and marine reserves for the high seas.

A government wants CO2 emissions to fall. Tax or cap and trade? S2 · 10 marks

Tax fixes the price and lets the market determine emissions (uncertainty: how much abatement). Cap fixes quantity and lets the market set the permit price (uncertainty: cost). Both can be efficient with full information; with unknown MACs the question is which mistake is costlier.

CO2 is a stock pollutant: one more tonne today creates no discontinuity, so the marginal damage curve is fairly flat. If abatement proves more expensive than expected, tolerating slightly more emissions is cheap. That favours a tax (Weitzman). Contrast a fishery near collapse, where steep damages favour a cap.

Political economy: a tax raises revenue (Mankiw: return it via tax cuts or rebates; harmonised international tax to bring in India/China); a cap with free permits hands rents to firms and invites lobbying (EU ETS Phase 1 over-allocation, price near zero; 2013 slump to ~€3-5). But caps give quantity certainty and, once reformed (verified baselines, auctioning, MSR), EU ETS exceeded €80. Scale check from Session 1: at the 2% consensus rate the carbon price is about $190/t, which on petrol (2.35 kg CO2/L) means ~₹42/L, taking the pump price from ₹100 to ₹142, which is why India resists and asks for finance. Session 2 adds: a government may like the tax's revenue; under a cap, connected firms win free permits.

Recommend: a carbon price, ideally a tax or a cap with auctioning and a price floor/reserve, backed by strong MRV (Session 3).

Can Coase solve Delhi's stubble-burning pollution? S2 · 8 marks

Coase says the efficient outcome follows from clear rights and costless bargaining, whoever holds the right. Option 1 (farmers may burn, Delhi pays them not to) fails because compliance is hard to verify and millions of citizens cannot bargain with millions of farmers. Option 2 (Delhi has a right to clean air, farmers pay to burn) is easier to monitor, since fires are visible by satellite, but whom does a farmer pay? Transaction costs are prohibitive, so pure Coase fails. Contrast Vittel, where the key number was 40 farmers, not the €200.

Reality: a government penalty (₹5k/₹10k/₹30k by farm size), a Coase-flavoured recognition of the right to clean air without a negotiated price. Farmers pay the government, not the people harmed. Enforcement is weak (2023: ₹2.4 crore imposed on 9,258 farmers, 11% recovered; farmers even time burning around satellite passes) but fires fell from 36,663 (2023) to 10,909 (2024), though in class the cause was called unclear. Complementary measures: NTPC buying stubble pellets, shorter-duration rice varieties. Link to Session 3: raise p (satellites) and a credible F; consider subsidising alternatives (Happy Seeder, straw markets) since farmers' MAC is the obstacle.

"The HFC-23 episode shows carbon markets cannot work." Discuss. S3 · 10 marks

Facts. HFC-23 (waste from HCFC-22 refrigerant) has a GWP ~11,700. Destroying it cost under €1/tCO2e but earned 11,700 CDM credits per tonne, worth over €100,000 at €10-20. Plants increased refrigerant output to make more waste to destroy; credit revenue exceeded refrigerant revenue; 19 plants (11 China, 5 India) captured about half of early CDM credits; waste ratios sat at the allowed maximum and fell when crediting stopped.

Diagnosis. Not a failure of markets as such but of contract design: an endogenous baseline (the agent controlled the quantity it was paid to destroy) creating moral hazard, and "endogenous baselines make moral hazard worse". The counterfactual was knowable: the professor's question was why the CDM did not pay against verified pre-CDM production data. So the design was fixable. Capture followed: windfalls funded resistance to reform and threats to vent.

Counter-evidence. US SO2 trading cut emissions cheaply; reformed EU ETS reached >€80 with falling power emissions.

Fixes. Pay against a verified pre-policy baseline; third-party governance; benchmark on technology; mandate destruction when abatement is this cheap (maybe with some compensation), just as catastrophic risks like nuclear waste get mandates; UN capped creditable waste at 1% of output and the EU banned the credits. Conclusion: markets work when what is rewarded cannot be manipulated by the agent.

Your firm has a net-zero target. Design incentives that avoid the failures from Session 3. S3 · 15 marks

  2.   3. Multi-task: balanced scorecard (emissions + safety + quality), keep any single incentive weak.
  4. Boundaries: targets on Scopes 1, 2 and 3 (Scope 3 is 70-90%); recalculate after divestment or outsourcing.
  5. Verification: do not pay for unobservable baselines; buy offsets only with central baselines and matched controls; pay assurers a fixed fee, not per credit.
  6. Enforcement: make gain < p×F: continuous monitoring, unannounced supplier audits, real bonus consequences.

What is additionality, and why are forest offsets prone to fail it? S3 · 8 marks

Additionality asks whether claimed abatement would have happened anyway. It requires a counterfactual that is never observed. REDD+ credits = claimed baseline deforestation minus actual. Developers forecast the baseline; with payment rising in the baseline, they choose high baselines (e.g. Madre de Dios: reference area included a road and a town with fast deforestation). Certifiers paid per credit (Verra, 10 cents) have weak incentives to challenge, which ended in a scandal. The logic is a drug trial without a fair placebo group: roads let machinery in and logs out, so a road-heavy control area inflates the baseline. Only the gap between a matched control and actual is justified. This is information asymmetry: adverse selection before (agent knows its true baseline) and unverifiable outcomes after. Fixes: do not pay for unobservable baselines; fixed-fee certifiers; central baselines (Verra 2023, though the pay problem may remain); a government baseline-setter where institutions are strong (capture risk); buyers who refuse unverifiable offsets; satellite and matched-control verification.

Why has the consensus discount rate fallen from 3% to about 2%, and what would a 2% rate mean at India's petrol pumps? S1 · 10 marks

Why 2%. Stern revised his 1.4% up, Nordhaus came well down from 5.5%, and the consensus moved from 3% to about 2% for three reasons from class. (1) Taxes: historic real returns of 2 to 3% are partly a transfer to government, so the tax-adjusted rate is lower. (2) Growth is endogenous: if we fail to protect the climate we will not grow as fast, so history's 2.3% overstates the future (the circularity problem). (3) Uncertainty: the 2.3% average includes the Industrial Revolution. Unsure between 2% and 3%, average the PVs, not the rates; 0.5/1.02100 + 0.5/1.03100 gives about 2.38%, and over 200 or 500 years it moves even closer to 2%.

At the pump. At 2% the carbon price is about $190 per tonne of CO2. One litre of petrol = 750 g × 85% ≈ 640 g of carbon × 44/12 ≈ 2.35 kg of CO2. So the tax is about $0.45 ≈ ₹42 per litre, pushing petrol from ₹100 to ~₹142. At 3% it would be ~₹11; at Nordhaus's 5.5%, ~₹1.

Link. This is why India's negotiators (Jaishankar) resist and ask rich countries to pay: in Ramsey terms India's higher g means a higher r.

Use the Ramsey equation to explain the India vs Europe split in climate negotiations. S1 · 8 marks

r = δ + ηg. Countries disagree mainly on g. India grows fast, so its r is higher and each rupee of abatement today costs more in welfare; it needs hospitals, growth and productivity now. Europe grows slowly, so a lower r and a stronger case for carbon taxes and green subsidies. India's stance (Jaishankar): you grew for a century without worrying about climate; you did the damage; if you want us to abate, subsidise us. Europe's reply: go green and we will support you, for example with carbon credits. The $190/t carbon price (₹42/L on petrol) shows what is at stake. The professor's conclusion: every Paris-style negotiation ultimately reduces to agreeing on δ, η and g. Ethical extras (colonial history) are a different argument from discounting. Add Rawls: behind a veil of ignorance you would not accept a deal that makes the poorer party carry the cost of the richer party's past emissions.

Why did the Vittel deal work, and would the same Coasean bargain work in rural India? S2 · 8 marks

Coase: clear rights plus cheap bargaining lead to the efficient outcome whoever holds the right. Vittel paid about 40 farmers €200 per hectare per year to change practices so nitrates stayed out of its spring. It worked because the key number was 40: few parties, a few days of meetings, a measurable outcome. "Costless" bargaining only has to be cheap enough. The contract also needed monitoring (annual runoff tests); without it the company keeps paying, the water stays polluted and trust collapses. In rural India with thousands of smallholders, contested rights (groundwater under land is really open access) and hard-to-observe behaviour, transaction and monitoring costs explode, so Coase fails. That is why stubble burning is handled with government fines instead, a "flavour of Coase" without a negotiated price, and why groundwater is capped indirectly through electricity rationing.

What is time inconsistency, and how can a government or firm make a sustainability commitment credible? S3 · 8 marks

Time inconsistency: the principal announces a rule it will want to abandon later, and agents know it, so nobody responds. Examples: a sustainability budget in the discretionary pool, cut in the first bad quarter; Yamuna clean-up pledges; school candidates promising air-conditioning with no budget. The reverse also holds: a credible promise changes behaviour before it binds. If the UNFCCC credibly said Scope 3 targets will be enforced in 5 years, firms would switch to cleaner suppliers now. Credibility comes from institutions: bodies with a legal basis that take decisions out of the election cycle (the UK Infrastructure Commission, despite some political appointees); checks and balances that bind the principal (Congress's power of the purse, courts striking down laws); inside firms, independent directors who question bad decisions. Pair this with enforcement: spend on p (tamper-proof monitors, well-incentivised third-party verification) and keep F credible and graded, since an "infinite" shutdown penalty is never used.

Using the four lenses, how should a company decide its sustainability priorities? R7 · 8 marks

Materiality matrices produce bloated lists (avg 11.8 top issues across 256 reports; experts suggest six or fewer). Screen issues through business value (bottom line: Walmart zero waste), stakeholder influence (what people will do: Eversource and methane leaks), science and technology (what data says: LCA shows coffee pods efficient), and purpose (what is ours to do: Costco's supply-chain workers). Prioritise only issues in all four. KDP went from 21 issues to 3 and launched plastic-free K-Rounds. Hold two tensions: outside-in vs inside-out, perception vs reality. Link to Session 3: fewer, well-chosen metrics reduce multi-task distortion.

"Sustainability is a cost burden." Refute using Nidumolu, Prahalad & Rangaswami. R8 · 8 marks

30-company study: sustainability lowers costs (fewer inputs) and raises revenue (better products, new businesses), so "Sustainability = Innovation". Five stages: (1) compliance as opportunity: HP's early lead-free solder and the European Recycling Platform saved $100 m+; (2) value chains: FedEx cut fuel 36% with new 757s; Cisco turned returns into a $100 m profit centre; IBM telecommuting saved $700 m/yr; (3) products: P&G cold-water Tide, Clorox Green Works (40% of a $200 m market); (4) business models: FedEx-Kinko's, Waste Management; (5) next-practice platforms: smart grid. Rules: start from the future, learn before investing, keep goals fixed but tactics flexible, collaborate. Caveat: benefits need capabilities built over a decade and the right internal incentives (Session 3).

Traps that cost marks

Session 1
  • δ = 0 does not mean r = 0 (the ηg term remains).
  • Higher η means a higher r and less abatement (egalitarian paradox).
  • Under uncertainty average PVs, never rates.
  • W&S do not say "ignore the future": they say raise savings, choose projects at market rates.
  • Positivists discount money, not lives.
  • ₹42/L is the carbon tax implied by a 2% rate ($190/t), not India's existing fuel tax.
  • Nordhaus: write 1.5 + 2×2 = 5.5% (the 2.3% historic growth doesn't add up to 5.5).
  • $100 tn ÷ 256 is about $0.4 trillion (billions, not millions).
Session 2
  • Negative externality = over-supply (not under-supply).
  • Coase: allocation of rights changes who pays, not the efficient quantity.
  • Pigouvian tax = marginal external damage, not a punishment or sin tax.
  • Tax fixes price; cap fixes quantity. Don't swap which is uncertain.
  • Cap and trade minimises cost by equalising MACs, not by equal cuts. The cap cuts pollution; the trade cuts cost.
  • Vittel's key number is 40 farmers, not €200.
  • A mandate (catastrophic or near-free abatement) is not the same as a uniform standard.
Session 3
  • HFC-23 counterfactual was not hidden; the failure was behaviour change (moral hazard), not missing data.
  • Moral hazard = hidden action after contract; adverse selection = hidden information before.
  • Additionality is about the counterfactual, not about measurement accuracy of actual emissions.
  • Becker: raising F only works while F is credible; an infinite F (shutdown) lowers effective p. p and F are complements.
  • India's NDC: capacity (50%) is not generation (~20%).
