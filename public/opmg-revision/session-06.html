Station 00 / Foundations

The Philosophy

Lean isn't a toolbox you bolt onto a process. It's a business system for organizing product development, operations, suppliers, and customer relations, aimed at one thing: matching what you produce to what the customer actually wants, using less of everything to get there.

Two things it optimizes for

Every lean tool traces back to two goals working together:

  • Synchronization of all flows with customer demand — build what's needed, not what's convenient to build.
  • At the lowest possible cost — less human effort, less space, less capital, less time, fewer defects.
Intuition Think of it like cooking a meal exactly as guests arrive, hot, fresh, portioned to appetite, instead of cooking a huge batch at 3pm and letting it sit under a heat lamp all evening. Same ingredients, wildly different amount of waste.
FIG 00.1 — the two pillars of lean

Waste = the gap

Lean defines waste very precisely: the gap between the performance of an ideal process and the actual process. Anything that doesn't move the product closer to what the customer wants is waste, even if it feels productive.

Where we're headed Six tools do the actual work of closing that gap: cut batch sizes, pull instead of push, build quality at the source, use flexible resources, redesign layout into cells, and reduce variability. Each has its own station on this page.

Why capacity doesn't move but flow time explodes

In steady state, a station can still finish one batch every 4 minutes, so throughput stays at 60 units/hour no matter the batch size. But a single unit now has to wait for its whole batch to accumulate at every stage before moving on — that waiting is pure queue time, and Little's Law (I = R × T) says that queue must show up as extra inventory sitting in the system.

So why batch at all? If changeovers between jobs are slow or expensive, splitting into single units means paying that setup cost constantly. That's the real trade-off: batch size shrinks toward one only as fast as you can shrink setup time. That's exactly what SMED (Single-Minute Exchange of Die) targets — make changeovers so fast that small batches stop being expensive.
Station 02 / Lean Tool #2

Pull, not Push — and JIT

Same three boxes — Supplier, Process, Customer — but the trigger for production flips depending on which philosophy you run.

Material shows up → station produces, whether or not the next step is ready.

Downstream need shows up → only then does the station produce.

FIG 02.1 — what actually triggers the process to run

The factory-floor version

Push: as soon as cloth arrives at the cutting station, cutting starts, because material is sitting there and the machine is free. It doesn't check whether sewing (downstream) actually needs those pieces yet. Cut pieces pile up in front of sewing.

Pull: sewing only asks cutting for more once it has actually used up what it had. Cutting only cuts when that request arrives. Nothing gets made until there's a real, immediate need for it.

JIT is what pull looks like in practice

JIT (Just-In-Time) = have exactly what's needed, how much is needed, when it's needed, where it's needed. A "hand-to-mouth" flow of material — nothing early, nothing extra, nothing sitting around "just in case."

Needed by whom? In JIT/pull, every station treats the station right after it as its customer. The trigger always comes from whoever consumes the output next, not from a forecast made in advance.

Rule of the card No card, no production. Station i is not allowed to make another unit until a card physically (or digitally) arrives authorizing it — which only happens once i+1 has actually consumed one. That single rule is what keeps WIP from silently piling up between stations.
Station 04 / Lean Tool #3

Quality at the Source — Jidoka

The radical version of Jidoka Stop the production line the instant a defect is found — until the root cause is identified, even if it isn't fixed yet. It feels like it kills output. It's actually the cheap option, because every stage a defect travels through multiplies the cost of the fix.

Think of it like a spell-checker that flags a typo the second you type it, versus one that only checks after you've printed and mailed 500 copies. Same typo — wildly different cleanup bill.

Station 05 / Lean Tool #4

Flexible Resources

Workers who can only do one task lock your capacity to whatever that one station allows. Cross-trained workers can flow to wherever the backlog actually is — the Kristen Cookie Co. idea.

Worker 1 — Mixing

Mixing dough

Worker 2 — Baking

Waiting on oven

Rigid team: each worker sticks to one task, even when the other task has a backlog.

Three payoffs

  • More capacity — a flexible worker shifts to whichever step is backed up right now, instead of standing idle.
  • More learning — rotating through stations means you actually see how your work affects the next person's, which is exactly what surfaces kaizen ideas.
  • Better job satisfaction — the same 60-second task all day is numbing; variety keeps people engaged.
Station 06 / Lean Tool #5

Functional → Cellular Layout

Group machines by what they are, and parts zig-zag across the whole factory. Group machines by what they build, and parts flow straight through.
FIG 06.1 — crisscrossing travel between departments
FIG 06.2 — one dedicated cell, straight-through flow
Why it matters In the functional layout, every part has to travel to wherever its next operation lives, waiting in queues at each shared department along the way. A cell puts every operation a product family needs, in sequence, right next to each other — no crisscrossing, no shared-resource queues, and it's much easier to spot a problem the moment it happens (which ties straight back to Jidoka).
Station 07 / Continuous Improvement

Kaizen & the Iceberg

Inventory is water. Problems are rocks. High water hides the rocks — which feels safe, until you realize you're sailing over a reef.
Missed due dates Late deliveries Scrap & rework Too much paperwork Engineering change orders Poor quality 100% inspection Long queues Machine downtime Too much space
Drag the slider down to lower the water and expose the rocks

Kaizen in three moves

  • Increase problem visibility — deliberately cut inventory (exploratory stress) so hidden problems surface.
  • Targeted improvements — active worker involvement, dedicated time to experiment, suppliers included.
  • Root cause analysis — ask "why" five times, fix the root cause not the symptom, then change, monitor, and adjust.
Station 08 / Lean beyond the factory

Time is Inventory, in Services

A service business usually can't stockpile a haircut or a coffee for later. So the thing lean tries to cut isn't shelf inventory — it's how long the customer waits.
ManufacturingServices
Physical inventory buffers problemsWait time / queue buffers problems
Cutting stock exposes defects, downtimeCutting wait exposes bottlenecks, understaffing
Little's Law: I = R × TSame law — T is now customer wait time
Field case — Starbucks A lean speed initiative shaved seconds off every order across 11,000+ stores. But one regular customer, who treated her Starbucks visit as a 20-minute escape rather than a transaction, felt rushed the moment staff started hurrying her order along — and left "bothered, but didn't know why."
The catch Speed is only "good operations" if it's what the customer actually values. This loops all the way back to Station 00: good operations means capabilities aligned with your strategic position — for some customers that's speed, for others it's the unhurried experience itself. Lean optimizes flow, not any one fixed definition of "better."
