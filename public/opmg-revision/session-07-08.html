Station 00 / Before you start

Stats Refresher

Everything from here on leans on four ideas from your intro stats course. If any of these feel rusty, spend two minutes here, each one links forward to exactly where it gets used.

1 · The normal curve

Most business measurements (delivery times, deformation under force, defect counts over many units) cluster around a typical value and spread out symmetrically on either side. That shape is the normal distribution, the bell curve. It's fully described by just two numbers:

  • Mean (μ) — the center of the curve, the "typical" value.
  • Standard deviation (σ) — how spread out the values are around that center. A small σ means most values sit close to the mean; a large σ means they're scattered widely.
FIG 00.1 — the two numbers that define a bell curve

2 · The empirical rule (68 – 95 – 99.7)

A quick sanity-check that's worth memorizing: for any normal distribution, a fixed share of values always falls within a fixed number of standard deviations of the mean.

68.27% of values fall within ±1σ of the mean
Where this reappears Station 03 (Process Capability) is this exact idea, just applied to a business spec limit instead of a generic ±kσ band, and Station 10 (Six-Sigma) is entirely about what happens to that shaded tail as σ shrinks.

3 · Z-scores: distance from the mean, in σ-units

A z-score answers one question: "how many standard deviations away from the mean is this value?" It's the universal translator that lets you compare a delivery time, a ball's deformation, or a factory's defect rate on the same scale.

z = (x − μ) / σ
z = 1.11 → 86.8% of values fall below this point
Where this reappears This converter IS the engine behind sigma capability (Station 03) and the p-value in Station 05 (Testing for Improvement). Everything downstream just plugs different numbers into this same z ↔ probability relationship, default shown here (z=1.11) is Grab-a-Bite's actual delivery-time sigma capability.

4 · Standard error: why bigger samples are steadier

A single observation can swing a lot. But the average of many observations swings much less, and shrinks predictably as the sample gets bigger. That shrunk spread is called the standard error, and it's the standard deviation of individuals divided by the square root of the sample size.

One more thing: one-sided vs. two-sided

Some specs only care about one direction, "30 minutes or less" only has an upper limit; nothing stops a delivery from being fast. Others care about both directions, a bolt's diameter has to stay above a minimum AND below a maximum. Watch for which kind you're dealing with, it changes whether you use one tail of the curve or both.

Station 01 / Foundations

Two Dimensions of Quality

Before any statistics, this session splits "quality" into two different questions. Confusing them is the single most common way to misread a J.D. Power-style rating.
FIG 00.1 — designing the right thing vs. building it consistently

Two separate questions

  • Quality of Design (Performance): did we choose the right features, materials, and attributes in the first place? A car with a weak engine can be built with zero defects and still be a "low quality" car by this measure.
  • Quality of Process (Conformance): once a design is chosen, does every unit that comes off the line actually match that design? A superbly designed car that randomly fails to start is low quality by this measure.
Field note — J.D. Power ratings J.D. Power splits its awards exactly along this line: "Quality and Reliability" ratings track conformance, how consistently a car meets expectations over months of ownership, while "Performance" ratings track design, the actual driving experience and features. A car can win one and not the other.
Where this session goes The rest of this manual is entirely about the second dimension, quality of process (conformance). We'll turn "does it meet the spec?" into hard numbers: process capability, statistical tests for improvement, and control charts to keep a process honest over time.
Station 02 / Where specs come from

Voice of the Customer

A specification is just the customer's expectation, translated into a number with a tolerance. Get the number wrong, and you're either wasting money on precision nobody notices, or shipping something customers can feel is off.

Same product, two very different tolerances

FIG 01.1 — deformation under 18 lb force, tight vs. loose tolerance

Both balls deform by roughly the same amount on average. The difference is the ± tolerance: the tournament ball allows only ±0.0025 inches of variation around the target, about 14× tighter than the everyday ball's ±0.035.

Why bother At the professional level, tiny inconsistencies in bounce are noticeable and can decide a point. For casual play, nobody can feel a difference of a few hundredths of an inch. The tighter spec isn't "better" in the abstract, it's calibrated to what that specific customer can actually perceive.

Specs can get very granular: the M&M's example

FIG 01.2 — target color mix per package, as a specification
Jerry Seinfeld, on the same idea "Only a seven-year-old kid can taste the difference between a red M&M and a light brown M&M... your red is more of a main course M&M, but the brown is more of a mellower flavor; it's an after-dinner M&M, really." The joke lands precisely because it's absurd, yet the company still specs the color mix to the percentage point, because for some customers, even a seemingly trivial attribute is part of what they're paying for.

Station 04 / Two levers, one target

What Counts as "Improvement"?

With a one-sided spec, there are exactly two ways to push more of the distribution inside the line, and a third option that does both at once.
FIG 03.1 — fixed spec line on the right; shaded = outside spec
—
Proportion outside spec
  • Mean reduction — shift the whole distribution away from the limit. Same spread, but centered further from danger.
  • Variability reduction — narrow the spread around the same mean. Same center, tighter consistency.
  • Both — shift and narrow together. This is the expensive-but-best option, and usually where six-sigma-style programs are aimed.

Station 06 / Don't mix these up

Specification Limits vs. Control Limits

Two lines that both look like boundaries on a chart, answering two completely different questions.
Specification limits What does the customer want? How often can we meet that requirement? This is a measure of how good the process is, set from outside, by the customer.
Control limits Is the process behaving the way it's "supposed" to, day to day? This is a measure of how stable the process is, set from inside, from the process's own historical variation.
The trap A process can be perfectly "in control" (stable, predictable, no surprises) while still being incapable of meeting the customer's spec, it's just reliably missing the target. Conversely, a process can occasionally meet spec while being wildly unstable underneath. Control tells you about consistency; capability tells you about meeting expectations. You need both.

Station 08 / The trade-off in setting limits

Type I vs. Type II Error

Control limits are a judgment call by the process manager, not a fact derived from the data. Move them tighter or looser, and you're trading one kind of mistake for the other.
Type I errorType II error
Cause Process hasn't actually changed, but this sample happened to look unusual (a false alarm) Process HAS actually changed, but this sample happened to look ordinary (a missed signal)
Consequence Out-of-pocket cost of investigating a root cause that isn't really there Opportunity cost of letting a broken process keep running, undetected
Widen or narrow the control band, and... Narrower control limits catch real problems faster, but trigger more false alarms (more Type I). Wider control limits reduce false alarms, but let real problems run longer before anyone notices (more Type II). The standard ±3σ convention is simply a commonly accepted balance point, not a law of nature.
Station 09 / Reading the shape of a chart

Patterns in Control Charts

The dots on a control chart aren't just in-control or out-of-control. The shape they trace tells you what kind of cause to go looking for.

Normal

Random jitter, comfortably inside the band. Cause: ordinary common-cause variation.

Lack of stability

Wild, erratic swings, some piercing the band. Cause: assignable/special causes, tool, material, operator, or over-adjustment.

Cumulative trend

A steady climb or decline over many points. Cause: tool wear or gradual drift, something is slowly degrading.

Cyclical

Regular up-down waves. Cause: shift changes, voltage fluctuations, seasonal effects, anything that repeats on a schedule.

The point of "six sigma" A process with a smaller standard deviation has more margin to spare, so when (not if) the mean eventually drifts, it takes much more drift before defects start appearing. A high-sigma process isn't just "cleaner today," it's more forgiving of tomorrow's inevitable disturbances.
