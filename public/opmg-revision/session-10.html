Station 00 / Foundations

Building Blocks & the Sawtooth

Every inventory-ordering decision boils down to five symbols. Once you can name them, the sawtooth diagram below explains itself.
  • R — flow rate: demand per unit time
  • Q — batch (order) size: our decision variable
  • r — opportunity cost of capital tied up in inventory
  • h — physical holding cost (storage, spoilage, etc.)
  • H = h + r — total cost of holding one unit for one unit of time
  • C — variable cost per unit
  • S — fixed cost per batch, incurred no matter how big the order is
FIG 00.1 — inventory profile: rises by Q on delivery, drains at rate R, repeats every Q/R
Fixed cost/time = S × (R/Q)  ·  Holding cost/time = H × (Q/2)  ·  Variable cost/time = C × R
Reading the sawtooth Every time a batch of Q arrives, inventory jumps up by Q, then drains steadily at the demand rate R until it hits zero, right as the next batch arrives. One full cycle takes Q/R time units. Average inventory over a cycle is just the average height of that triangle, Q/2.
Station 01 / Economies of scale

Deriving the EOQ

Two costs move in opposite directions as Q grows. The optimal batch size, the EOQ, sits exactly where their sum is smallest.
FIG 01.1 — fixed cost (falling), holding cost (rising), total cost (U-shaped)
QEOQ = √(2RS / H)   ·   Ordering frequency = R / Q

The headline result If demand quadruples, it's optimal to double the batch size AND order twice as often, batch size and frequency share the extra demand, neither absorbs it all. To cut batch size in half without touching demand, the fixed cost S must fall by a factor of 4.

Station 04 / The simple case first

Reorder Point Under Certainty

With perfectly steady, known demand, there's a clean rule: place the next order exactly when remaining inventory equals what you'll sell during the lead time, no more, no less.
FIG 04.1 — inventory hits exactly zero the instant the new order arrives
ROP = R × L

The Reorder Point (ROP) is the trigger: whenever on-hand inventory drops to this level, place a new order. Under deterministic demand, that new order arrives just as the shelf reaches empty, cutting it as close as possible with zero risk, because there's no uncertainty to guard against.

Station 06 / Sizing the cushion

Lead-Time Demand & Safety Stock

Demand during the lead time is really the SUM of many days (or weeks) of random demand. That sum's own uncertainty is what safety stock has to cover.

Why the sum's spread grows as √L, not L

If each period's demand is independent with variance σ², then summing L periods adds up the variances: total variance = L×σ². Standard deviation is the square root of variance, so it comes along at σ×√L, not σ×L. Some high periods, some low periods, they partially cancel, which is exactly why the spread grows slower than the total itself.

LTD ~ N(R×L, σ×√L)   ·   Safety stock = z × σ × √L   ·   ROP = R×L + Safety stock

Station 08 / The playbook

Drivers of Effective Inventory Management

Every real lever companies pull traces back to one formula: safety stock = z × σ × √L. There are only two knobs to turn, σ and L.
Safety stock = z × σ × √L
Reduces σ

Aggregation — Products

Postponement: delay the product-specific decision (color, configuration) as late as possible, so uncertainty is pooled across many variants instead of borne by each one. Asian Paints' in-store tinting system and Dell's build-to-order model both work this way.

Reduces σ

Aggregation — Geography

Physical consolidation into fewer, larger warehouses pools regional demand swings into one calmer combined stream (Amazon, Bigbasket, Lenskart). Information sharing across the chain, better visibility into real demand, reduces the effective forecast error even without moving inventory at all.

Reduces L

Colocation of Suppliers

Physically locating suppliers near the point of use shrinks lead time directly. Zara's nearby manufacturing lets it restock stores in days, not months. Toyota's supplier parks sitting right next to assembly plants are what make JIT delivery of parts feasible at all.

Connecting back Colocation of suppliers is the same idea behind "pull" and JIT: less distance and time between trigger and delivery means less uncertainty to buffer against, and therefore less safety stock needed in the first place.
Station 09 / When the model's assumptions break

Case: Inventory Management During COVID

Even famously lean, Just-In-Time companies keep some safety stock. Why didn't that stock protect the world's supply chains during the pandemic?
Ordinary disruptions A single supplier's factory fire, a storm closing one port, a short strike, these are localized and temporary. Safety stock is built exactly for this: it bridges a short gap while the system finds a workaround, switches suppliers, or waits out the disruption.
COVID-19 A persistent, GLOBAL disruption hitting nearly every link in the chain at once, for years, not weeks. Extra inventory couldn't prevent the problem, it could only delay when the shortage became visible. Once that buffer ran out, the underlying disruption was still there.
The lesson Safety stock is a buffer sized for the kind of uncertainty it was designed for, short, localized, and roughly normally distributed. A slow-moving, structural, worldwide disruption is a completely different kind of risk, and no amount of extra σ×√L math fixes a problem that isn't statistical noise around a stable mean.

Quick Reference

Cheat Sheet

Every formula from this session, compressed for the margin of an exam.

Building blocks

  • R=demand rate, Q=batch size, S=fixed cost/batch
  • H=h+r=holding cost/unit/time
  • C=variable cost/unit

EOQ

Q* = √(2RS/H)
  • Ordering freq = R/Q
  • At Q*, fixed cost = holding cost

Square-root scaling

  • Demand ×k → optimal Q ×√k
  • Halve Q → S must fall by 4×

ROP, certain demand

ROP = R × L
  • Inventory hits zero exactly as new order arrives

Lead-time demand

LTD ~ N(RL, σ√L)
  • Variances add over L periods, std. dev. grows as √L

Safety stock & ROP

SS = z·σ·√L  ·  ROP = RL + SS
  • Avg inventory = Q/2 + SS

Pooling (any flavor)

  • Pooled SS = z·(σ√n)·√L
  • Cuts total SS by factor 1/√n
  • Works across locations OR products (postponement)

Worked answers

  • Kristen's raw material EOQ ≈ 510 dozen
  • Northern Airline Q* ≈ 53 people, ≈9–10 batches/yr
  • Coffee house EOQ ≈ 229 lbs, ROP ≈ 16 lbs
  • Fun Colors: 8,700 → ≈1,000 cans pooled

Two levers, one formula

  • Aggregation (products or geography) → reduces σ
  • Colocation of suppliers → reduces L
