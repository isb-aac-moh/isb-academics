Station 00 / Before you start

Concepts Refresher

This session leans on two ideas: expected value, and cumulative probability. Both are quick to rebuild if they've gone rusty.

1 · Expected value: a weighted average of outcomes

If an outcome pays you $A with probability p, and a different outcome costs you $B with probability (1−p), the expected value is just each payoff multiplied by its chance of happening, added together.

Expected value = (A × p) + (−B × (1−p))
Win $1.25 with 50% chance, lose $0.25 with 50% chance → expected value = $0.50
Where this reappears Station 03 turns "should I stock one more unit?" into exactly this kind of bet, win a profit margin if it sells, lose a small amount if it doesn't.

2 · Cumulative probability: "how much has piled up by here?"

P(D ≤ Q) just means: add up the probabilities of every demand value from the smallest possible, up through Q. Drag Q and watch the shaded bars accumulate.

P(D ≤ 10) = 50.0% — the shaded bars are every outcome at or below Q
Where this reappears Every "optimal quantity" answer in this manual comes from finding the Q where this cumulative number first crosses a target percentage, the "optimal service level" from Station 04 onward.
Station 01 / Kristen's Cookies, one more time

The Setup

Kristen and her roommate now sell freshly baked chocolate chip cookies, by the dozen, and never sell yesterday's batch. Any dozen left at closing gets dumped at a steep discount in the last hour.

The economics

  • Retail price: $2.49 / dozen
  • Production cost: $1.24 / dozen
  • Leftover (salvage) price: $0.99 / dozen, sold in the last hour
  • Mean daily demand: 10.5 dozen
The catch They've hired help to run things for three days while they're away at a case competition. The help needs to know exactly how many dozens to bake each morning, before that day's demand is known, and cannot bake more mid-day. Demand is random each night, but the underlying distribution doesn't change day to day.

Reading the curve Expected profit keeps climbing past the mean of 10.5, because the two kinds of mistakes aren't equally painful. Understocking (running out) forfeits a full $1.25 of margin per missed dozen. Overstocking (leftovers) only costs $0.25 per unsold dozen. Since running out is 5× more expensive than a leftover, it pays to lean past the average, right up until the peak, where one more dozen stops being worth the risk.
Station 03 / The logic behind the peak

The Marginal Unit & the Fair Bet

Forget the whole curve. Ask one question at a time: "I've decided to stock Q dozen, should I stock one more?"
  • If that extra dozen sells (chance it does = P(D>Q)): you earn price − cost = $2.49 − $1.24 = $1.25
  • If it doesn't sell (chance it doesn't = P(D≤Q)): you earn salvage − cost = $0.99 − $1.24 = −$0.25

That's a bet: win $1.25 with some probability, lose $0.25 the rest of the time. Drag the slider to set how likely the extra dozen is to actually sell, and watch when the bet stops being worth taking.

At 50% chance of selling, expected value of stocking one more = +$0.63 — worth it
Verdict Positive expected value — stock the extra dozen.
The breakeven point This bet is exactly fair when P(D>Q) = Co / (Cu+Co) = 0.25 / 1.50 ≈ 16.7% (about 1 in 6). Below that, the extra dozen is more likely to become a $0.25 loss than a $1.25 win, so stop adding units. That threshold, flipped around, is exactly the newsvendor rule: stock until P(D≤Q) reaches 100% − 16.7% = 83.3%.

Station 05 / Same numbers, different demand shape

Same Ratio, Different Shape

Scones use the identical price, cost, and salvage as cookies, and even the same mean demand (10.5). Only the SHAPE of the demand distribution differs, cookie demand clusters near the mean; scone demand is spread evenly across the whole range.
FIG 05.1 — cookies: peaked demand → Q* = 13
FIG 05.2 — scones: flat (uniform) demand → Q* = 16
The lesson The optimal SERVICE LEVEL (83.3%) only depends on the economics, Cu and Co, so it's identical for both products. The optimal QUANTITY depends on that service level AND the specific shape of the demand distribution, so it lands on 13 for cookies but 16 for scones, even with the same mean and same margins. Never assume Q* transfers across products just because the price and cost do.

Station 07 / Not just cupcakes and scones

Case: Hotel California

A luxury hotel, always fully booked via advance reservations at $200/night. New manager Rocky starts holding back rooms for last-minute walk-ins at a premium $300/night. He currently holds back 3 rooms. Is that optimal?

Mapping this onto Cu and Co

Here, "stocking" means deciding how many rooms to hold back from advance booking. "Demand" is the number of last-minute requests that show up.

  • Understocking (held back too few rooms): a walk-in wanted a room at $300 but none was reserved, that room instead sold in advance for only $200. Lost margin: Cu = $300 − $200 = $100.
  • Overstocking (held back too many rooms): a reserved room never got a late request, and now sits empty, forfeiting the guaranteed $200 advance sale entirely. Co = $200.

The newsvendor math says otherwise here, because a full empty room (Co=$200) is a much bigger loss than the $100 upside of catching one more late request.

Station 09 / Beyond cookies and hotel rooms

Real-World Applications

Any time a quantity must be committed before demand is known, and getting it wrong in either direction has a real cost, this exact Cu/Co logic applies.

Fashion retailing

Online fashion retailers use the newsvendor model to allocate inventory depth across warehouses and sizes before a season's real demand is known, balancing markdown risk against stockout risk at massive scale.

Healthcare

Hospitals apply the same structure to reserving operating room time, blocking out a fixed amount of OR capacity in advance, against uncertain future surgical demand.

Agriculture

Seed producers plan seed-corn production a full growing season ahead of the farmers who will actually plant it, committing to quantities long before real orders arrive.

The pattern to recognize Whenever you hear "we have to decide how much to make/buy/reserve before we know exactly how much will be needed, and being wrong in either direction costs something," that's a newsvendor problem. Find Cu, find Co, compute the ratio, and you have a defensible answer.

Quick Reference

Cheat Sheet

Every definition and formula from this session, compressed for the margin of an exam.

The newsvendor problem

  • Commit to Q in advance
  • Demand D is random
  • Cannot adjust once D is known (unless Quick Response applies)

Cost of understocking

Cu = price − cost (r − c)
  • Opportunity cost of not stocking one more unit that would have sold

Cost of overstocking

Co = cost − salvage (c − s)
  • Out-of-pocket cost of a unit stocked but not demanded

Optimal service level

SL* = Cu / (Cu + Co)
  • "Newsvendor ratio" or critical ratio
  • Depends only on economics, not on demand shape

Optimal quantity

Q* = smallest Q with P(D≤Q) ≥ SL*
  • Depends on BOTH the service level AND the demand distribution's shape

Kristen's Cookies numbers

  • Cu=$1.25, Co=$0.25, SL*=83.3%
  • Q*=13 dozen (cookies), 16 dozen (scones)

Sensitivity rules

  • Cost ↑ → Cu ↓, Co ↑ → order less
  • Free leftovers → Co ↑ → order less
  • Perfect info → no mismatch cost, order = demand

Hotel California

  • Cu=$100, Co=$200, SL*=33.3%
  • Optimal hold-back = 2 rooms, not 3

Quick response

  • Stockout ≠ fully lost sale anymore
  • Cu shrinks → lower optimal service level → order less upfront

Know which model you're in

  • Newsvendor: ONE order, ONE period, can't reorder — this session
  • EOQ / safety stock / ROP: ONGOING replenishment, many cycles, can reorder — Session 10
