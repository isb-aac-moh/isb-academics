Station 00

Did Operations Save $10,600?

Target's gross margin fell from about 30% to 21.5% in two quarters while inventory ran 40% above the prior year. Was that a demand failure, a pricing failure or an execution failure? Variance analysis is the machinery for answering exactly that.

The claim to reject"Total costs came in at $78,400 against a budget of $89,000 — production saved us $10,600. Great execution." Volume was 20% below plan. Of course costs were lower.
What actually happenedAgainst a flexible budget of $74,000 at 8,000 units, the plant overspent by $4,400. The saving was an artefact of the comparison.

Vocabulary

  • A variance is actual minus budget
  • Favourable (F) means better than budget; unfavourable (U) means worse
  • Variances are always in money, never in kilograms or hours — money makes their relative importance comparable
Station 01

The Flexible Budget

The budget re-computed at the actual output volume: what would revenue and costs have been at the volume achieved, if everything else had gone as planned?
Static budget → Flexible budget → Actual results
ColumnVolumePrices and standards
Static (master) budgetBudgetedBudgeted
Flexible budgetActualBudgeted
Actual resultsActualActual
One input from realityThe flexible budget takes exactly one number from the actual results — the volume. Everything else comes from the original standards. Contaminating it with an actual price destroys the separation it exists to create.

Station 02

The Decomposition Tree

Every variance question is somewhere on this tree. Draw it before answering anything.
Every named variance sits somewhere on this tree
Total profit variance = sales volume variance + flexible budget variance
Sales volume variance = flexible budget profit − master budget profit
Sales price variance = actual revenue − flexible budget revenue
Fixed cost spending variance = budgeted fixed cost − actual fixed cost

Reading the split as a diagnosis

Glass Vessels' revenue shortfall of $32,000 against the static budget splits $24,000 to volume and $8,000 to price. Both sit with the revenue centre and mean different things: one is a forecasting or market problem, the other is discounting discipline or pricing power.

Back-solvingExam questions often give you all but one component and ask for the missing one. Write the identity, sign every term with F positive and U negative, and solve.
Station 03

Splitting a Variable Cost Variance

Labour cost came in over budget. Two possible reasons: more input was used than the standard allows, or each unit of input cost more. Three columns separate them.
ColumnWhat it isName
BQ × BPStandard input for the actual output, at standard priceFlexible budget
AQ × BPActual input used, at standard priceThe "as if" budget
AQ × APActual input, at actual priceActual
Quantity (efficiency) variance = (AQ − BQ) × BP
Price (spending) variance = (AP − BP) × AQ
BQ = actual output × standard input per unit of output
Worked: Glass Vessels labourStandard 0.4 hours at $10; output 8,000 units; actual 4,400 hours costing $34,000. BQ = 3,200 hours, so the flexible budget is $32,000. As-if = 4,400 × $10 = $44,000. Quantity variance $12,000 U, price variance $10,000 F, netting to the $2,000 U on the line.
Worked: materialStandard 0.5 kg at $6; output 8,000; actual 4,500 kg costing $25,500. BQ = 4,000 kg → flexible $24,000; as-if $27,000. Quantity $3,000 U, price $1,500 F, total $1,500 U.

Running it backwards

Given the variances and asked for AQ and AP, fill the three columns in order.

  • Flexible budget is computable from the standards and the actual output
  • Step to the as-if column using the quantity variance, then divide by BP to recover AQ
  • Step to actual using the price variance, then divide by AQ to recover AP
Worked: problem 8.42Price variance $3,150 U, quantity variance $4,500 F, flexible quantity 18,000 kg at $1.50. Flexible = $27,000. As-if = 27,000 − 4,500 = $22,500 → AQ = 15,000 kg. Actual = 22,500 + 3,150 = $25,650 → AP = $1.71. Total material variance $1,350 F.
TrapBQ is not the original budget's input quantity. It is the input the standard allows for the output actually produced. Using the master budget's quantity here is the most common error in the topic.
Station 04

Signals, Not Verdicts

A favourable variance is not automatically good news, and four numbers on one page usually tell one story rather than four.

One story, three variances

VarianceGlass Vessels
Material price$1,500 F
Material quantity$3,000 U
Labour price$10,000 F
Labour quantity$12,000 U
Read separatelyPurchasing looks excellent and production looks incompetent.
Read togetherCheaper, lower-grade material and cheaper, less experienced labour generated waste and took longer. Investigate the purchasing decision first, not the plant.

What each variance points at

VarianceQuestion it raises
Sales pricePricing power? Competitive pressure? Discounting discipline?
Sales volumeForecasting failure? Market shift?
Labour efficiencyProcess improvement? Automation? Training?
Material priceSupplier negotiation? A quality trade-off?
Fixed costCapacity discipline? A structural change?

Gaming

  • Cheap inputs — buy lower quality for a favourable price variance
  • Deferred maintenance — postpone spending to hit a short-term target
  • Budget slack — pad so the standard is soft
  • Short-termism — sacrifice long-term investment for this period's number
Unfavourable can be rightJason's putter: a $50,000 U fixed cost variance from renting a milling machine bought $100,800 F of material quantity and $56,000 F of labour quantity — a net gain of $106,800.

When variance analysis is less useful

  • High fixed-cost environments — software, semiconductors. Variable cost variances are immaterial.
  • Rapidly shifting demand — the budget is obsolete before the period ends
  • Innovation-driven firms — variance discipline discourages experimentation
  • Service and knowledge work — honest quantity standards are hard to define

Buckeye National Bank

The service-sector version of Session 6. Buckeye chased retail accounts, paid branch managers for account growth, hit the targets, and watched profits fall for three straight years.

Why the numbers moveBusiness customers write four times the check value, which the old base charged them for. What actually costs money is walking into a branch and telephoning — and retail does 80% of the teller transactions and 95% of the calls.
What Buckeye should doNot stop serving retail. Fix the bonus plan first — paying for account count with no view of cost-to-serve pays managers to add loss-making accounts. Then: minimum balances and per-transaction fees, push routine activity to ATM and online channels, and target the retail segments that behave like the profitable accounts.
Caution on the ratesTen more teller customers will not add $60 of cost tomorrow. The rates are long-run average capacity costs — right for a strategic question, wrong for a short-run incremental one.

Cheat Sheet

Everything this session can be examined on, compressed to what fits on one screen.

Three columns

  • Static: budgeted volume, budgeted standards
  • Flexible: actual volume, budgeted standards
  • Actual: everything actual

Top of the tree

Total = sales volume + flexible budget
Volume = flexible profit − master profit
Flex = actual profit − flexible profit

Inside the flex variance

Sales price = actual rev − flexible rev
Fixed = budgeted FC − actual FC
  • Plus one variance per variable input

Input split

Quantity = (AQ − BQ) × BP
Price = (AP − BP) × AQ
BQ = actual output × standard per unit

Glass Vessels numbers

  • Static $31,000, flexible $22,000, actual $9,600
  • Volume $9,000 U, flex $12,400 U
  • Labour: qty $12,000 U, price $10,000 F
  • Material: qty $3,000 U, price $1,500 F

Running it backwards

  • Flexible from the standards
  • Step by the quantity variance → as-if → ÷ BP = AQ
  • Step by the price variance → actual → ÷ AQ = AP
