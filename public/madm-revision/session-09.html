Station 00

Measuring a Division That Owns Assets

How an investment centre is scored, and why the choice of measure changes what its manager does. This never got its own deck, and it is in the assigned reading and four assigned problems.
ROI = operating income ÷ assets employed
Residual income = operating income − (required return × assets)
EVA = operating income × (1 − t) − WACC × (assets − current liabilities)
WACC = (1 − t) × wd × rd + we × re

Two opposite distortions

MeasureDistortionWorked example
Profit aloneOver-investment — the manager gets the benefit without being charged for the capitalA 12% project is accepted against a 15% cost of capital
ROIUnder-investment — any project below the current ROI dilutes the ratio26% on $300,000, plus $5,000 income on $50,000 of assets → ROI falls to 23.71%, so she declines
Residual incomeNot comparable across sizes — a big division has a big number by constructionEvery project above the charge raises it, so the incentive is right
EVAComplex to implement, particularly the GAAP adjustmentsAfter-tax income, WACC charge, assets net of current liabilities
TrapA high ROI can mean a small denominator. A business relying on personal service rather than modern facilities has old, heavily depreciated assets, so its book asset base is small and its ROI flattering — 35.71% in the textbook case.
Owner versus managerOwners take the long view and accept a project that dilutes this year's ROI. A hired manager evaluated on year-over-year ROI does not. That gap is goal incongruence, and performance measures narrow it without ever closing it.
Station 01

What a Transfer Price Is

When one division sells to another inside the same firm, the internal price is the transfer price. It is revenue to the seller, cost to the buyer, and exactly zero-sum for the group.
For the selling divisionThe transfer price is revenue. A higher price raises its reported profit.
For the buying divisionThe transfer price is a cost. A higher price lowers its reported profit.
The consequenceInternal transfer prices do not change corporate pre-tax profit. They shift profit between divisions.

So why the heat?

  • Bonuses and promotions — divisional profit is the primary metric for evaluating a division manager
  • Capital allocation — profitable divisions get investment, unprofitable ones get cut
  • Sourcing decisions — make or buy hinges on the price the buying manager faces
  • Divisional survival — a persistently unprofitable division gets shut or sold

Three questions in every problem

  • Decision rights — may divisions source externally when internal supply exists, and who sets the price?
  • Goal congruence — what price leads autonomous managers, each maximising their own profit, to do what is best for the firm?
  • Sourcing choice — from the corporate perspective, is an internal transfer better than an external purchase at all?
Three objectives, in tensionGoal alignment, divisional autonomy, fair performance measurement. No system achieves all three.
TrapThe zero-sum property holds for pre-tax profit within one tax jurisdiction. Cross borders and the price starts moving the group's tax bill.
Station 02

The Minimum and Maximum Rule

Almost every transfer pricing question reduces to two numbers.
Min TP (seller) = incremental cost + opportunity cost of the transfer
Max TP (buyer) = external purchase price ± costs saved or incurred by buying internally
The corporate test is the same two numbersThe seller's minimum is the true cost of making in-house. The buyer's maximum is the true cost of buying. Compare them and the sourcing decision follows.
No zone is an answerIf the minimum exceeds the maximum, no price exists that both divisions accept — and that is the correct signal that the firm should buy outside, not a negotiation failure.

Adjustments on each side

ChangeEffectExample
Extra work to make the transferred unitRaises the seller's floorTesla's $600 modification: $4,000 → $4,600
Costs the buyer avoids by sourcing insideRaises the buyer's ceiling$60 of inspection and freight: $1,000 → $1,060

TrapUnitised fixed cost is never in the minimum transfer price. It is sunk with respect to the transfer decision, and it enters only when a corporate policy forces it in — which is Session 10's cautionary tale.
CaveatThis framework is for short-run decisions. Divisions will not sign long-term commitments at a price covering only incremental and opportunity cost, because over the long run capacity itself is a choice.

Capacity Is the Whole Story

The seller's opportunity cost is the entire content of the transfer price, and capacity determines it.
SituationOpportunity cost per unitMinimum TP
Excess capacityZero — nothing is displacedVariable cost

A constrained resource

Division A runs two products on one machine with 600 hours a month. Product 1: demand 300 units, price $10, variable cost $4, one hour each. Product 2: unlimited demand, price $15, variable cost $7, two hours each.

  • Rank by contribution per machine hour: Product 1 earns $6, Product 2 earns $4
  • Optimal mix: Product 1 at 300 units (300 hours), then Product 2 at 150 units (300 hours)
  • Transferring 50 units of Product 1 uses 50 hours → displaces 25 units of Product 2 → 25 × $8 = $200, or $4 per transferred unit
  • Min TP = $4 + $4 = $8 against Division B's external price of $9
TrapRank by contribution per unit of the constraint, not per unit of product. Product 2 has the higher margin per unit and the lower margin per machine hour, and the machine is what is scarce. The unitised fixed costs of $2 and $6 are there to be ignored.
