Station 00

Choosing a Policy

Why have a policy at all? Because resolving disputes case by case pulls top management in constantly, undermines the decentralisation the firm chose, eats executive time, and asks the centre to arbitrate with local information it does not have.
PolicyStrengthWeaknessShare of firms
Market-basedObjective, and equals the right answer at full capacityNeeds a competitive external market to exist~30%
NegotiatedPreserves autonomy; uses the divisions' own informationHaggling, conflict, bargaining-power outcomes~24%
Cost-basedSimple to administer, always availableDistorts sourcing whenever sourcing is delegated~46%

Cost-based splits into variable cost 5%, full cost 24% and cost-plus 17%.

The rule that falls outCost-based policies are workable when sourcing is mandated internally, because then the price only moves reported profit and cannot change a decision. Once sourcing is delegated, negotiated prices are the better default.
Station 01

The Full-Cost-Plus Trap

The worked example the session turns on, and the practice final tests it directly.

The setup

  • Divisions may source where they like; internal transfers are priced at the seller's budgeted full cost plus 10%
  • Division A's budgeted full cost is $350 — $150 variable, $200 fixed unitised over budgeted volume
  • A's capacity is 100,000 units and it can sell only 70,000 externally
  • Division B needs 20,000 units and can buy outside at $300

What Division B sees

InternalExternal
Cost per unit$385$300
Total for 20,000$7.7M$6.0M
B's choiceBuy external. Any manager evaluated on divisional profit would.

What the company sees

  • A has idle capacity, so the true incremental cost of internal supply is $150
  • Buying outside spends $300 to avoid a $150 cost
  • Loss = $150 a unit × 20,000 = $3 million
DiagnosisThe manager is rational. The policy is at fault.
Two features caused itUnitised fixed cost that is not incremental, and a mark-up that is pure internal profit. The buying division treats the sum of both as its real cost of internal supply.
Station 02

Seth, Brenda and Holdup

Seth's components division designed a custom part with Brenda's refrigerator division, spending $240,000 on design and mock-ups on nothing firmer than an understanding that he would be the front-runner in the bidding.

The positions

SethBid $230 for 20,000 units a year — full cost plus about 15%. Variable cost $125, allocated overhead $75. Running at 70% capacity, missed bonus last year.
BrendaOutside bids around $170. Offers $200 to preserve the relationship and Seth's engineering support. Values his quality.

The arithmetic, per unit, against buying outside at $170

Transfer price$170$200$230
Change in Seth's profit$45$75$105
Change in Brenda's profit$0−$30−$60
Change in corporate profit$45$45$45
The only thing that matters to corporate$45 a unit — $900,000 a year on 20,000 units — at every transfer price. The value comes from making the part at $125 rather than buying at $170. The price only decides who gets credit.
And the zoneFloor is Seth's variable cost of $125 (he has spare capacity, so nothing is displaced). Ceiling is Brenda's $170. His $230 bid is outside any range she would rationally accept.

Should corporate intervene?

ForResolves the conflict quickly. Ensures the value-creating transfer actually happens.
AgainstUndermines decentralisation. Time-consuming, and HQ lacks the local information. Sets a precedent that every future dispute gets escalated.

The holdup problem

Seth's real grievance is not the price. He sank $240,000 into an asset with value only in this relationship, on a vague promise, and Brenda now holds the bargaining power. Relationship-specific investment + incomplete commitment = holdup.

Apple and GT Advanced, 2014GT invested hundreds of millions in sapphire capacity for Apple. Apple pulled back. GT went bankrupt.
The remedies are all ex anteA long-term purchase commitment or take-or-pay clause; cost sharing so the buyer co-funds the investment; pre-agreed formula pricing or arbitration; vertical integration or an internal sourcing mandate. Nothing works after the money is sunk.
Station 03

International Transfer Pricing

Once divisions sit in different tax jurisdictions the price stops being zero-sum for the group, because subsidiaries are taxed where they earn income.

The two levers

  • Shift profits to low-tax countries by setting low transfer prices on goods sold to those subsidiaries
  • Load costs into high-tax countries by purchasing from low-tax affiliates at inflated prices

Drag it

Parts cost $40 in the US at 21%; assembly costs $20; the product sells for $100. The transfer price is the price at which the US entity sells the part to the assembly subsidiary.

What stops it

Arm's-length pricingMost jurisdictions — the US, India, the OECD countries — require the transfer price to reflect what unrelated parties would charge. Acceptable methods: comparable uncontrolled price, cost-plus, profit-split, and advance pricing agreements negotiated with the authority in advance.
OECD Pillar TwoA 15% global minimum corporate tax from 2024 for groups above €750 million of revenue. If a subsidiary's effective rate falls below 15%, the parent's jurisdiction imposes a top-up. It caps the gain from profit shifting without ending transfer pricing disputes.
Why intangiblesA factory cannot be moved to a tax haven; a patent portfolio or a brand can be transferred with a signature. The royalty then runs from the operating country, which takes the deduction, to the haven, which books the income — and there is rarely a comparable market price to benchmark against.

Apple and the Double Irish

  • IP licensing income routed through two Irish entities with different tax residencies
  • Non-US profits flowed to entities with minimal taxable presence; effective rates on some Irish profits reached 0.005%
  • 2016: the European Commission ruled Ireland had granted illegal state aid, ordering €13 billion in back taxes
  • 2024: the Court of Justice upheld it. Ireland closed the structure to new entrants in 2015

Google's Bermuda arrangement, Starbucks' Dutch royalty structure, Amazon's Luxembourg operations, and the IRS disputes with Coca-Cola and Medtronic are all versions of the same argument. All involve intangibles.

Station 04

Closing the Course

What the transfer pricing module adds up to, and the four things he asked the class to remember from the whole of his half.

Transfer pricing summary

  • Transfer prices drive behaviour. They set divisional profit, which drives bonuses, capital allocation and survival.
  • The right price depends on context, and opportunity cost is what context means. Full capacity points to market price; excess capacity points to variable cost.
  • No policy is perfect. Match the policy to the situation — mandated sourcing tolerates cost-based prices, delegated sourcing needs negotiation or market prices.
  • Relationship-specific investment creates holdup risk, and the remedy is contractual and comes first.
  • International transfer pricing is a tax question bounded by arm's-length rules and the global minimum tax.

The four parting thoughts

01

  • There is no such thing as the cost of something.

02

  • Numbers make people do things.

03

04
