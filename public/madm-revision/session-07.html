Station 00

Not All Customers Are Equal

The same ABC logic, pointed at customers instead of products. The result is a whale curve: cumulative profit rises steeply, peaks well above 100%, then falls back as the unprofitable tail eats into it.

What makes a customer expensive to serve

  • Many small orders rather than a few large ones
  • Order changes after placement, and expedited deliveries
  • Heavy support and inquiry usage
  • Custom specifications and returns
  • Long payment terms and discounts taken
Customer profit = revenue − variable cost − Σ (activity rate × units consumed)

Station 01

Managing the Tail

A quarter of customers generate nearly all the profit and the bottom fifteen per cent destroy a fifth of it. "Fire them" is the answer the numbers seem to shout, and it is rarely the right first move.

The menu, in the order to try it

  • Reprice. Service fees, order minimums, menu pricing that charges for the activities the customer consumes. This converts a cost problem into a customer decision.
  • Change how you serve them. Self-service, digital channels, standard catalogue items. Cost-to-serve falls without the revenue leaving.
  • Change how they order. Work with them on their ordering pattern, and share how the profitable customer runs purchasing.
  • Change nothing, and ask what the numbers are missing. Referrals, reference value, a loss-leading product that pulls a profitable one, an account being deliberately developed.
Why firing is the last resortThe revenue and contribution leave immediately. Much of the assigned cost is capacity cost that stays — and it is then spread across fewer customers, which makes the next tranche look unprofitable and invites another cut.
TieringABC-based customer profitability lets a firm tailor service levels and prices by tier, and answer three questions with evidence: which customers to acquire, which to support and retain, which to let go.

From costing to managing

Activity-based management is what you do with the visibility. Six questions, in order:

#QuestionWhat it buys
1What are our high-cost activities?Visibility into where the money goes
2Do they add value for the customer?Separates value-add from waste
3Can we eliminate or simplify them?Process redesign
4Can we do them more efficiently?Continuous improvement
5Should someone else do them?Outsourcing and partnerships
6Can technology eliminate them?Automation and digital channels
Cutting cost vs removing its causeCutting a department's budget cuts capacity while the demand for the work stays. Removing an activity, or halving how often it is triggered, removes the demand — which is the only version that holds.
Station 02

Decentralisation and Control

Decision rights get distributed as firms grow. Everything that follows in this course is a response to what that costs.

Benefits

  • Quicker decisions by people with local expertise
  • Better adaptation to local conditions
  • Higher morale from empowerment
  • Frees top management for strategy
  • Develops the future leadership bench

Costs

  • Coordination failures across units
  • Duplication of activities and expertise
  • Goal incongruence
  • Silo behaviour

Management control systems

Processes and structures that keep activity pointed at the firm's goals. The objective is goal congruence: making the choice that is best for the manager also the choice that is best for the firm.

  • Allocation of decision rights — who decides what
  • Choice of performance measures — are the metrics consistent with those rights?
  • Choice of reward systems — how measured performance is paid
Controllability principleTo the extent possible, hold employees accountable only for outcomes they control. It is a guideline rather than a law — almost nothing is entirely within one person's control — and it is what motivates the flexible budget in Session 8.

Station 03

Building the Budget

A quantified plan that allocates resources and sets expectations. It is three things at once, and that overlap causes everything in the next station.

Three things at once

  • A forecast — the best guess of what will happen
  • A contract — the resources committed
  • A target — the number you are judged against

Three roles

  • Planning — decide what to do, commit resources
  • Coordination — align functions, communicate expectations
  • Performance evaluation — hold managers to the plan
The tensionA good forecast and a tough target are rarely the same number.

The build order

  • Revenue budget — by customer, region and product. Triangulate: local knowledge, macro and industry trends, market research. Every downstream number inherits its error.
  • Production budget — the inventory equation, in units
  • Input budgets — materials from the bill of materials, labour from the routing sheet, overhead from the cost of capacity
  • COGM then COGS — the inventory equation again, in money
  • Period costs — selling and admin flow straight to the income statement
  • Budgeted income statement, then taxes
Production = sales + ending inventory − beginning inventory
Purchases = production × input per unit + ending RM − beginning RM
COGS = beginning FG + COGM − ending FG
WorkedBuildIT: 8,000 lots to sell, 90 wanted at the end, 75 on hand → 8,015 to produce. Producing 15 more than are sold means inventory rises, so variable COGS comes in lower than variable COGM.
Meridian, from the practice finalSales 20,000, opening FG 1,500, closing FG 2,500 → production 21,000. At 2 kg each that is 42,000 kg. Opening RM 2,000, closing RM 5,000 → purchases 45,000 kg. At $4 a kg, $180,000. The equation runs twice.

The cash budget

Ending cash = opening cash + collections − payments

Collections split the current period's sales from prior periods' receivables by the stated pattern; payments do the same for purchases. A profitable month can still need a loan.

Station 04

The Games People Play

The moment a budget is used to evaluate people, behaviour changes — because the same number now sets pay, status and next year's resources.

The ratchet effect

Most firms anchor next year's target on this year's actual. Beat your target and next year's is harder. So managers learn not to look too good, and there is a ceiling on how much anyone will over-deliver.

Participative budgeting: cure or cause?

BenefitsTaps local knowledge, builds buy-in and commitment.
CostsInformation asymmetry invites misrepresentation. The people closest to the numbers also benefit from shading them.

Most firms land on a mix of top-down and bottom-up, with goals set “tight but attainable”.

The four games

GameWhat is shadedWhen it happens
SandbaggingThe output or revenue targetWhen the target is negotiated
PaddingThe resources requestedWhen the budget is built
Spending to avoid lapsingNothing — unspent budget gets usedEnd of the financial year
Timing gamesWhich period a transaction lands inAround the period boundary
His framingThese are rational responses to how the budget is used, not bad apples. The fix is in the system, not in the individuals.

Design exercise: pay for accuracy?

Scrap budget-attainment bonuses and pay managers for forecast accuracy instead.

What it buysHonest numbers. No reason to sandbag or pad, because a low forecast is now one you have to hit exactly rather than a target you want to beat.
What it losesAny reason to stretch. And a manager heading for 110 against a forecast of 100 now has a reason to slow down.
