Activity-Based Costing

Why a firm's own cost system misleads it about which products make money, and what activity-based costing does about it. Traditional allocation, the cost hierarchy, cross-subsidy, and when ABC is worth building.

Station 00

Which Cost Matters

The session opens on something deliberately small — a copying machine — because it makes one point that runs through the whole of Prof. Dutta's half: the right cost number depends on the decision.

The facts

  • Copier costs $4,000 and lasts four years, so $1,000 a year
  • Ink cartridge costs $400 and lasts 40,000 pages, so $0.01 a page
  • Paper costs $0.01 a page
  • Wages and benefits $10,000 a year; other overhead $1,000 a year
  • Volume 600,000 pages a year

Two answers, both right

Variable cost = $0.01 + $0.01 = $0.02 per page
Full cost = $0.02 + $12,000 / 600,000 = $0.04 per page

The $12,000 is depreciation plus wages plus other overhead. Neither number is an estimate of the other; they answer different questions.

DecisionRelevant cost
One more page$0.02
One-off 10,000-page order into spare capacity$0.02
Should we run this function at all?$0.04
Outsource for five years at $0.03?$0.04 — so yes, outsource
The ruleShort run, capacity already in place → variable cost. Long run, capacity itself is a choice → full cost including capacity.
TrapThe distinction is not one-off versus repeat. It is whether the commitment puts capacity back in play. A standing order for a third of the machine's annual volume, for five years, is a long-run decision even though each page is still just a page.

Where the rate cannot see

Product B is 25% of the volume and places 50 of the 65 orders. Overhead in this business is largely triggered by how many separate times somebody has to schedule a run, process an order and ship it. A labour-hour rate has no way to represent order count at all.

Complexity, not volumeOrder processing, production scheduling, shipping paperwork, design changes, special tooling, quality testing, custom specifications, customer support and returns. None of these scale with units.
Station 02

The Cost Hierarchy

Four levels, sorted by what actually makes a cost happen one more time. This is the most reliably examined idea in the session.
LevelHappens once per…Typical costsDriver
Unitunit producedEnergy, machining, consumablesUnits, machine hours, labour hours
Batchrun, order or shipmentMachine setup, scheduling, order processing, shippingNumber of setups, orders, shipments
Productproduct line kept aliveDesign specs and updates, product-specific toolingNumber of products, often split equally
Facilityyear in businessRent, plant insurance, general administrationNone — usually left unallocated

How to classify anything

Ask what has to happen one more time for the cost to be incurred again. One more unit, one more run, one more product line, or one more year in business.

WorkedA furniture maker re-programs its CNC machines at the start of every production run of a chair model. A run of ten and a run of a thousand each need it once, so the trigger is the run: batch level. Designing the chair model itself would be product level, because it happens once for the model however many runs follow.
Why it mattersLow-volume products consume batch- and product-level resources out of all proportion to their volume. A specialty product made in fifty small runs consumes fifty setups; a commodity product made in five large runs consumes five. A volume-based rate charges them by units and gets it exactly backwards.
Facility levelLeft unallocated for decision-making, because no driver reflects causation and no product manager controls them. Allocating them anyway loads every product with an arbitrary charge that can flip a ranking without carrying information.
Station 03

ABC Rebuilt

Products do not consume resources. They consume activities, and activities consume resources. So the assignment runs in two stages.

Three steps

  • Identify activities — what work actually gets done
  • Trace cost to activities — a separate pool per activity, not one bucket
  • Assign pools to cost objects — by a driver that causally moves the pool
Activity rate = pool ÷ total driver units   |   Assigned = Σ (rate × units consumed)

Station 04

The Cross-Subsidy Problem

Under a volume-based system, high-volume standard products are overcosted and low-volume custom products are undercosted. Two names for the same error, and both are dangerous.

Overcosted

High volume, standard, large routine orders. They appear less profitable than they are.

RiskManagers cut or over-price the product that is actually subsidising the rest.

Undercosted

Low volume, custom, small special orders. They appear more profitable than they are.

RiskUnprofitable products and customers proliferate.

Greg's lawns

Greg charges every customer the same price to mow a lawn. That is a volume-based allocation in disguise: it assumes every lawn takes the same work, when size, terrain and landscaping vary the time enormously.

  • Difficult lawns are underpriced, easy lawns overpriced
  • Easy-lawn owners get better quotes elsewhere and leave
  • Difficult-lawn owners find Greg cheap and arrive
  • The mix drifts toward the jobs that take longest, and average cost climbs while the price stays flat
The manufacturing versionA firm pricing from volume-based costs watches its mix shift away from easy-to-make high-volume products toward difficult low-volume ones — one rational customer decision at a time.

The death spiral

Drop the overcosted high-volume product and most of the allocation base leaves while the overhead largely stays. The rate rises, the remaining product's reported cost rises with it, and it starts to look unprofitable too.

Strategic dangerCompanies accumulate unprofitable products and customers while believing they are growing. Revenue rises, margins erode, and the cost system reports nothing wrong — because it was never measuring the thing that changed.
Station 05

When ABC Is Worth It

ABC produces better numbers and costs real money to build and maintain. The judgement is about where the gain justifies the cost.

Where it pays

  • Products or customers differ a lot in volume, batch size or support consumption
  • Overhead is large relative to direct cost
  • The firm is actively deciding what to keep, drop or reprice
  • Service businesses with several customer segments — a bank, a hospital, a hotel

Where it adds little

  • A cement plant with one commodity product — nothing to reallocate
  • A single-product contract manufacturer
  • Overhead that is a very small share of total cost
Diversity is the preconditionWithout it, every allocation method gives the same answer.

His implementation guidance, verbatim

  • Consider the costs and benefits of incremental precision
  • Focus on the low-hanging fruit first
  • Do not let the best become the enemy of the good
  • Better to be approximately right than grossly wrong
  • Start simple and look for scalability

A caution on the rates

Long-run average, not short-run incrementalAn activity rate is the long-run average cost of the capacity an activity consumes. It answers "what does this product line or customer segment cost us" well, and "what happens if ten more customers use a teller tomorrow" badly. In the short run that capacity is already paid for.

How AI may reshape the hierarchy

Batch level

Automated order intake, scheduling and documents make a small order nearly as cheap to process as a large one.

Product level

Generated design variants and automated compliance testing make a wider product range far cheaper to support.

Customer level

AI handling inquiries and personalising at scale makes the long tail of small customers viable.

New pools

Compute cost, model licensing, hallucination risk, data governance. ABC has to be extended to capture them.
